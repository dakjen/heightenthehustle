# Creating Clean, Readable Slides

> Use consistent fonts and colors, strong contrast, tidy alignment, and one main idea per slide.

## Why this matters

A slide has about three seconds to make sense. If the room is still reading it while you talk, nobody is listening to you. Cluttered slides also send a message you do not intend, which is that the business behind them is disorganized. Picture a mobile mechanic presenting to a company that runs a fleet of delivery vans. One slide holds his whole story in tiny type with a photo squeezed into the corner. The manager in the back row cannot read any of it. Now picture the same information on three slides with large type and plenty of empty space. Same facts, very different impression. Clean design is not about being artistic. It is about following a short list of rules.

## What you'll learn

- How to limit a deck to one or two fonts and a small set of colors
- How to choose text and background colors with strong contrast
- How to line things up so a slide looks tidy
- How to keep each slide to one main idea
- How to redesign a cluttered slide step by step

## The lesson

### Rule one: consistent fonts

Use one font for the whole deck, or two at the most, with one for titles and one for body text. Keep the sizes the same from slide to slide. Titles should be 36 points or larger. Body text should be at least 24 points. If you have to shrink the text below 24 to make it fit, you have too many words, not too little space.

The easiest way to stay consistent is to type in the layout placeholders, because they already share a font and size. To set the look for the whole deck at once, go to the **Design** tab and pick a simple theme. A **theme** is a matched set of fonts, colors, and backgrounds. Choose a plain one. Busy backgrounds fight with your words.

### Rule two: consistent colors

Pick two or three colors and stop. A good set is one dark color for text, one light color for the background, and one accent color for the things you want to stand out. If your business already has colors on its sign, shirts, or cards, use those. Use the accent color the same way every time, such as for titles only. When color is used at random, it stops meaning anything.

### Rule three: strong contrast

**Contrast** is the difference between the text color and the background color. High contrast is easy to read. Low contrast is not. Dark text on a light background works. Light text on a dark background works. Yellow on white, gray on light gray, and red on blue do not.

Remember that a projector in a bright room washes colors out. What looks fine on your laptop may vanish on the wall. Here is a quick test: step back six feet from your screen and squint. If you can still read every word, your contrast and your text size are good. Also be careful about putting words on top of a photo. If you must, place a solid-colored shape behind the words first.

### Rule four: alignment

**Alignment** means things line up along the same invisible edge. When titles start at the same spot on every slide and the left edges of your text and pictures match, the deck looks calm and planned. When things are a little off, people notice even if they cannot say why.

1. Left-align body text. Centered paragraphs are harder to read. Centering is fine for a short title.
2. When you drag an object, watch for the thin guide lines that appear. They show when it lines up with something else on the slide. Let go when the line appears.
3. To line up several objects exactly, click the first, hold **Shift**, and click the others. Then on the **Home** tab, click **Arrange**, choose **Align**, and pick **Align Left** or **Align Top**.
4. Leave empty space around the edges. Do not push text or pictures right up to the border of the slide.

### Rule five: one main idea per slide

Each slide should make one point. The title states the point. Everything else on the slide supports it. If you have three points, make three slides. Slides are free.

Keep the words short. Aim for no more than four or five lines, and no more than about six words per line. Write phrases, not full sentences. The slide is a signpost. You are the one who explains it. Put the full explanation in the notes pane below the slide, where only you can see it.

### A redesign, step by step

Here is a cluttered slide for Reliable Wrench Mobile Mechanics, a fictional business. It has a title in one font, a paragraph in another, light gray text, and everything is centered.

Title: `About Us and Our Services and Why Choose Us`

Body: `Reliable Wrench Mobile Mechanics has been serving the area since 2019 and we come to your home or job site so you never have to wait at a shop. We do oil changes, brakes, batteries, and diagnostics and we are fully insured and we offer fleet discounts for companies with five or more vehicles.`

To fix it, first find the ideas. There are three: who they are, what they do, and why to choose them. That makes three slides.

- **Slide 1, title:** `We Come to You`. **Body:** `Mobile mechanics since 2019` and `Home or job site service`.
- **Slide 2, title:** `What We Fix`. **Body:** `Oil changes`, `Brakes`, `Batteries`, `Diagnostics`.
- **Slide 3, title:** `Why Fleets Choose Us`. **Body:** `Fully insured`, `No waiting at a shop`, `Discounts for 5 or more vehicles`.

Then apply the rules. One font. Dark text on a white background. Titles in one accent color. Body text left-aligned at 28 points.

### In Google Slides

To change the theme, go to **Slide > Change theme** and choose a simple one from the panel. Guide lines appear when you drag objects, just as in PowerPoint. To align several objects, select them, then go to **Arrange > Align**. Font and size controls are in the toolbar above the slide.

## Try it

1. Open PowerPoint and start a new blank presentation. Save it as `Redesign-Practice`.
2. On the first slide, build the cluttered version. Type the long title and the full paragraph from the example above. Center it, make the text light gray, and shrink it until it fits.
3. Add three new slides with the **Title and Content** layout.
4. Build the three redesigned slides using the titles and short lines from the example.
5. Go to the **Design** tab and choose a plain theme with a light background.
6. Check that body text is at least 24 points and left-aligned on all three new slides.
7. Run the slide show. Compare the first slide with the three that follow. Step back six feet and squint at each one.
8. Save the file.

You are done when you have a saved deck with one cluttered slide and three clean ones, and you can say out loud which rules made the difference.

## Common mistakes

- **Shrinking the text to make it fit.** Fix: cut words or split the slide in two.
- **Using a different font or color on each slide.** Fix: apply one theme and type in the placeholders.
- **Light text on a light background.** Fix: switch to dark text, or darken the background.
- **Writing full sentences and reading them aloud.** Fix: cut to short phrases and move the full wording to the notes pane.
- **Centering everything.** Fix: left-align body text and keep centering for short titles.

## Check yourself

1. What is the smallest size body text should be?
2. What is contrast, and what is one example of poor contrast?
3. How many main ideas belong on one slide?
4. What should you do when your text does not fit?

## What you walk away with

A practical set of five design rules, covering fonts, colors, contrast, alignment, and one idea per slide, that you can apply to any presentation you make.
