# Getting Paid Faster

> Set up ways to accept money, prevent late payment before it starts, and save three follow-up scripts you can send the day an invoice goes overdue.

## Why this matters

Work you have finished but not been paid for is a loan you gave your customer. Small businesses cannot afford to be lenders. Take a caterer who finishes a $1,200 event, sends the invoice a week later because the next two events are already on her, accepts only checks, and has no set point for following up. Six weeks pass. She has already paid for the food and her helper. The job was profitable on paper and a cash drain in practice. Getting paid faster comes down to three things you control: how quickly a customer can pay you, what you agree to before the work, and what you do when the due date passes.

## What you'll be able to do

- Choose payment methods knowing what each costs you
- Weigh a processing fee against the cost of waiting
- Run three prevention habits that stop most late payments
- Work the follow-up ladder: 3 days, 2 weeks, 30 days
- Send any follow-up in one minute from a saved template

## The lesson

### Step one: set up ways to accept money

If a customer wants to pay you today and cannot, that delay is on your side of the table. You need at least one payment method set up, and most businesses do best with two.

| Method | How it works | Cost to you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zelle | Bank-to-bank transfer, instant | Free |
| Cash App or Venmo business account | Apps customers already know | Takes a cut of each payment |
| Square, Stripe, or QuickBooks Payments | Accept cards, looks most professional | Roughly 3% per transaction |

**Zelle** is best for clients you know, since the money moves straight from their bank to yours. **Cash App and Venmo business accounts** are familiar to customers, and they take a cut of each payment. **Square, Stripe, and QuickBooks Payments** let customers pay by card. They look the most professional and cost roughly 3% per transaction.

### The fee is the cost of getting paid now

Run the real numbers. On a $400 invoice, a fee of roughly 3% is about $12. You receive about $388 today. The other choice might be waiting 30 days for a check for the full $400. During those 30 days you still have rent, supplies, and your own bills. A 3% fee beats a 30-day wait.

That does not mean any fee is fine. Know each fee before you choose. Look up the current fee for any service before you sign up, because fees differ and they change. Treat the fee as a cost of doing business, record it as an expense, and account for it when you set prices. Then put your payment details on every invoice, so the customer never has to ask how to pay.

### Step two: prevention

Most late payments are decided before the work starts. Three habits prevent them.

**Take deposits on larger jobs.** A deposit, part of the price paid up front, covers your early costs and confirms the customer is committed. The caterer with the $1,200 event could ask for $400 before she shops, leaving $800 due after the event. If the customer goes quiet, she is waiting on $800, not $1,200, and her food costs are covered.

**Agree on terms before work starts.** Your terms are the price, the deposit, when the rest is due, and how to pay. Put them in writing, even in a short email or text the customer replies "yes" to. People pay on time more often when they agreed to the date in advance.

**Invoice the day the work ends.** The customer is happiest with your work the moment it is finished. Every day you wait, the job fades in their mind and your invoice drops lower on their list. Send it the same day.

### Step three: the follow-up ladder

Even with good prevention, some invoices will go past due. Most late payers are not refusing to pay. They are busy, and your invoice slipped. Following up is a routine part of collecting, the same as sending the invoice. The ladder has three steps, counted from the due date.

**3 days past due: the friendly nudge.**

"Hi [Name], I hope you're doing well. This is a quick reminder that invoice [number] for $[amount] was due on [date]. I've attached another copy for you. You can pay by [payment method and details]. Thank you so much!"

**2 weeks past due: the direct check-in.**

"Hi [Name], I'm following up on invoice [number] for $[amount], which is now two weeks past due. Can you let me know what day I should expect payment? If there's a problem with the invoice or you need to work something out, please tell me and we'll sort it out. You can pay by [payment method and details]."

**30 days past due: the firm final notice.**

"Hi [Name], invoice [number] for $[amount] is now 30 days past due. This is my final notice. Please send payment in full by [specific date]. I'm not able to schedule any further work until this balance is paid. You can pay today by [payment method and details]. Thank you for taking care of this."

Notice what changes. The tone moves from friendly to direct to firm. It never becomes angry or insulting. Every message states the invoice number, the amount, and how to pay. Polite and relentless gets paid.

### Save them before you need them

Follow-ups get put off when each one has to be written from scratch on a busy day. Write your three messages once, in your own voice, and save them as email templates. Then following up takes one minute. The day an invoice goes overdue, the message is ready and the decision is already made.

## Do this now

This is what everyone does together in the session.

1. Pick at least one payment method from the table and set it up now. Check its current fee first.
2. Add your payment details to the "how to pay" line on your invoice.
3. Write one sentence stating your deposit rule for larger jobs.
4. Write your standard terms: when payment is due and how customers can pay.
5. Personalize the three scripts so they sound like you.
6. Save each one as an email template named "Nudge, 3 days," "Check-in, 2 weeks," and "Final notice, 30 days."
7. List every invoice that is unpaid right now and send the script that matches how late it is.

You finish with a working payment method, your payment details on your invoice, and three follow-up templates saved and ready.

## Where owners lose time or money

- **Only one slow way to pay.** A customer ready to pay today waits until they find a checkbook. Fix: set up at least one instant method and list it on every invoice.
- **A payment service chosen without checking the fee.** The cut comes out of every sale from then on. Fix: know each fee before you choose.
- **A large job started with no deposit.** You carry the materials and the blocked date at your own risk. Fix: collect the deposit before you buy materials or block the date.
- **Days or weeks between finishing and invoicing.** Each day of delay is a day added to the wait. Fix: invoice the day the work ends.
- **One follow-up, then silence.** The customer reads silence as "no rush." Fix: follow the full ladder at 3 days, 2 weeks, and 30 days.

## Before you move on

- Which payment method did you set up, and what does it cost you per payment?
- At what job size do you require a deposit, and how much?
- Where are your three scripts saved, and can you send one in under a minute?
- How much are you owed right now, and which script does each unpaid invoice get today?

## What you walk away with

At least one live way to accept money, a deposit rule and payment terms in writing, and three follow-up scripts saved as templates.
