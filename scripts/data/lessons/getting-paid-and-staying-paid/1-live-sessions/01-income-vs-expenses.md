# Income vs. Expenses

> Did you make money last month? Not "did money come in." List your real last 30 days and find your rough profit.

## Why this matters

Most owners can tell you what came in last month. Very few can tell you what they kept. Those are two different numbers, and only one of them pays your rent. Picture Tasha, who runs a house cleaning business. She had her busiest month ever and deposited $3,800. She felt rich until the account was nearly empty on the 28th. Nothing was stolen. She had simply never added up what the business cost her to run. Once you can answer "did I make money last month?" with a real number, every other money decision gets easier.

## What you'll learn

- The plain meaning of income, expense, and profit
- How to list your real last 30 days of money in and money out
- How to calculate rough profit in a few minutes
- Why forgotten subscriptions and small charges matter
- The separation rule: one business bank account, starting now

## The lesson

### Three words you need

**Income** is every dollar customers paid your business. It is also called revenue or sales. A loan, a gift from family, or money you moved in from your own savings is not income, because nobody paid you for work.

An **expense** is every dollar the business spent in order to operate. Supplies, gas for work trips, booth rent, software, insurance, and fees your card reader takes are all expenses.

**Profit** is what is left when you subtract expenses from income. Profit is the number your business lives on. If income is big and profit is small, you are busy but not getting ahead.

The math never changes:

Income - Expenses = Profit

### "Money came in" is not "I made money"

Money coming in feels like success, and that feeling is the trap. A $600 job that cost you $450 in materials and a helper made you $150, not $600. If you price, spend, and pay yourself based on the $600, you will run short and not understand why.

The fix is not complicated. You look at the money that came in, you look at the money that went out, and you subtract. You do it for one full month so that monthly bills are included.

### A worked example: Tasha's month

Here is Tasha's real last 30 days, pulled from her bank app and her payment apps.

| Money in | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Regular home cleanings | $2,400 |
| Two move-out cleans | $600 |
| Office contract | $800 |
| **Total income** | **$3,800** |

| Money out | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Helper pay | $600 |
| Cleaning supplies | $310 |
| Gas | $240 |
| Insurance | $120 |
| Phone | $85 |
| Card processing fees | $41 |
| Scheduling app | $29 |
| Design app (forgot about it) | $15 |
| Cloud storage (forgot about it) | $10 |
| **Total expenses** | **$1,450** |

$3,800 - $1,450 = $2,350 profit.

Tasha made $2,350, not $3,800. That is still a good month. But now she knows her real number, and she found two subscriptions she did not remember signing up for.

### The forgotten charges always surface

When you scroll through 30 days line by line, small repeating charges show up. A free trial that turned into a monthly fee. An app you used once. A membership you meant to cancel. Each one looks tiny. Together they can add up to real money over a year. Tasha's two forgotten charges were $25 a month, which is $300 a year. You cannot cut what you cannot see.

### The separation rule

Tasha's hardest step was sorting business charges from groceries and streaming, because everything ran through one personal account. That is why this session ends with one rule: **one business bank account, starting now.**

When the business has its own account, every transaction in it is a business transaction by definition. You stop guessing. Your month takes minutes to add up instead of hours. Customers pay into that account, and business costs come out of it. When you need money for yourself, you move it to your personal account on purpose. You will learn how to do that the right way in the QuickBooks Essentials unit.

## Do this now

This is what everyone does together in the session.

1. Open your bank app and any payment apps you use, such as Cash App, Venmo, or Square.
2. Set the date range to the last 30 days.
3. On paper or in a note, make two columns: Money In and Money Out.
4. Go line by line. Write down every payment from a customer under Money In.
5. Write down every business cost under Money Out. Skip personal spending for now.
6. Circle any charge you forgot about or do not recognize.
7. Add up each column.
8. Subtract Money Out from Money In. Write the answer large and label it "Rough profit, last 30 days."
9. Write down the date you will open, or start using, a business-only bank account.

You finish with one number, your rough profit, and a date for separating your accounts.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting deposits as profit.** Fix: always subtract expenses before you call a month good or bad.
- **Counting loans or your own savings as income.** Fix: only money customers paid for your work goes in the income column.
- **Leaving out small or cash expenses.** Fix: go line by line through the bank app, and jot down cash spending the same day.
- **Mixing personal and business spending.** Fix: one business bank account, and nothing personal runs through it.
- **Guessing instead of looking.** Fix: use the real last 30 days from your bank app, not your memory.
