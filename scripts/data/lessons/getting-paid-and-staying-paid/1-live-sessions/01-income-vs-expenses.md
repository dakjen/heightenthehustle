# Find Out What You Kept Last Month

> Money coming in is not the same as money made. List your real last 30 days, subtract what the business cost to run, and get your rough profit.

## Why this matters

Most owners can tell you what came in last month. Far fewer can tell you what they kept. Those are two different numbers, and only one of them pays your rent. Take Tasha, who runs a house cleaning business. She had her busiest month ever and deposited $3,800. On the 28th the account was nearly empty. Nothing was stolen. She had spent the month doing the work, and nobody had totaled what the work cost to deliver. Once you can answer "did I make money last month?" with a real number, pricing, hiring, and paying yourself all rest on fact instead of feel.

## What you'll be able to do

- Sort your last 30 days into money customers paid you and money the business spent
- Keep loans, savings, and transfers out of your income number
- Calculate rough profit in a few minutes from your bank and payment apps
- Catch forgotten subscriptions and small repeating charges
- Run the separation rule: one business bank account, starting now

## The lesson

### Sort the money before you add it

The math never changes:

Income - Expenses = Profit

The number is only as good as what you put in each column, so sort with care.

**Income** is every dollar customers paid your business for work. You will also see it called revenue or sales. A loan, a gift from family, or money you moved in from your own savings is not income, because nobody paid you for work. Counting it makes a slow month look healthy.

An **expense** is every dollar the business spent in order to operate. Supplies, gas for work trips, booth rent, software, insurance, and the fees your card reader takes all count. Card fees are the one owners miss most, because the processor takes its cut before the deposit lands. Record what the customer paid as income and the fee as an expense, so you can see what accepting cards costs you.

**Profit** is what is left. It is the number the business lives on. Big income with small profit means you are busy and not getting ahead.

### "Money came in" is not "I made money"

A $600 job that cost you $450 in materials and a helper made you $150, not $600. If you price, spend, and pay yourself based on the $600, you run short, and the bank balance will not tell you why.

The fix takes minutes. Look at the money that came in, look at the money that went out, and subtract. Do it for one full month so that monthly bills are included.

### A worked example: Tasha's month

Here is Tasha's real last 30 days, pulled from her bank app and her payment apps.

| Money in | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Regular home cleanings | $2,400 |
| Two move-out cleans | $600 |
| Office contract | $800 |
| **Total income** | **$3,800** |

| Money out | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Helper pay | $600 |
| Cleaning supplies | $310 |
| Gas | $240 |
| Insurance | $120 |
| Phone | $85 |
| Card processing fees | $41 |
| Scheduling app | $29 |
| Design app (forgot about it) | $15 |
| Cloud storage (forgot about it) | $10 |
| **Total expenses** | **$1,450** |

$3,800 - $1,450 = $2,350 profit.

Tasha made $2,350, not $3,800. That is still a good month. Now she knows her real number, and she can see where the rest went. Helper pay, supplies, and gas were $1,150 of the $1,450. Those three rise with every job she adds, so they are the ones to watch when she quotes new work. She also found two subscriptions she no longer used.

### The forgotten charges always surface

When you scroll through 30 days line by line, small repeating charges show up. A free trial that turned into a monthly fee. An app you used once. A membership you meant to cancel. Each one looks small. Over a year they add up to real money. Tasha's two forgotten charges were $25 a month, which is $300 a year. You cannot cut what you cannot see.

A 30-day window has one blind spot: bills that hit once a year or once a quarter. If you pay insurance, a license, or a software plan annually, it may not appear in this month at all. Note those separately. A yearly bill divided by twelve is the true monthly cost, and it belongs in your thinking even in months when no money leaves the account.

### The separation rule

The slowest part of Tasha's hour was sorting business charges from groceries and streaming, because everything ran through one personal account. That is why this session ends with one rule: **one business bank account, starting now.**

When the business has its own account, every transaction in it is a business transaction by definition. Your month takes minutes to add up instead of hours. Customers pay into that account, and business costs come out of it. When you need money for yourself, you move it to your personal account on purpose, as a transfer you can see. The QuickBooks Essentials unit covers how to do that the right way.

A separate account also pays off outside your own records. A lender, a landlord, or a tax preparer who asks for statements gets clean business statements, and you are not handing over your grocery history to prove what the business earns.

## Do this now

This is what everyone does together in the session.

1. Open your bank app and any payment apps you use, such as Cash App, Venmo, or Square.
2. Set the date range to the last 30 days.
3. On paper or in a note, make two columns: Money In and Money Out.
4. Go line by line. Write down every payment from a customer under Money In.
5. Write down every business cost under Money Out. Skip personal spending for now.
6. Circle any charge you forgot about or do not recognize.
7. Add up each column.
8. Subtract Money Out from Money In. Write the answer large and label it "Rough profit, last 30 days."
9. Write down the date you will open, or start using, a business-only bank account.

You finish with one number, your rough profit, and a date for separating your accounts.

## Where owners lose time or money

- **Reading deposits as profit.** A big deposit month can still be a thin month. Fix: subtract expenses before you call a month good or bad.
- **Loans or your own savings counted as income.** It inflates the number you price and plan from. Fix: only money customers paid for your work goes in the income column.
- **Small and cash expenses left out.** They are the ones that never make it into the total. Fix: go line by line through the bank app, and note cash spending the same day.
- **Personal and business spending in one account.** Every month-end turns into a sorting job. Fix: one business bank account, and nothing personal runs through it.
- **Working from memory.** Memory keeps the big jobs and drops the $15 charges. Fix: use the real last 30 days from your bank app.

## Before you move on

- What was your rough profit for the last 30 days, to the dollar?
- Which of your expenses rise with every job, and which stay the same whether you are busy or slow?
- Which charge did you circle, and will you cancel it or keep it?
- Do you have a bill that only hits once a year, and what does it cost per month?
- On what date does your business money move into its own account?

## What you walk away with

Your real rough profit for the last 30 days, a list of charges you had forgotten, and a commitment to one business bank account.
