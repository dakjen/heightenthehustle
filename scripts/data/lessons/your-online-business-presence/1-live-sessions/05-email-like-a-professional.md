# Email Like a Professional

> Fix your address, build your signature, write emails that get answered, and save templates for the three hardest messages.

## Why this matters

Email is where the serious parts of business happen: quotes, contracts, invoices, complaints. People judge you by it before they ever meet you. Imagine Tanya, a caterer bidding on a company's holiday lunch. Her food is the best in the county, but her quote arrives from an address like `sparklequeen2004@`, with no subject line and no signature. The office manager is not sure it is real, and it sits unopened. A professional address and a clear message would have put her at the top of the pile.

## What you'll learn

- How to choose a business email address you will never be embarrassed by
- How to build a four-line signature
- Four rules for writing emails that get answered
- How to write the unhappy-customer reply, the quote that protects your scope, and the polite no

## The lesson

### Upgrade one: the address

Your email address is part of your name in business. An old personal address tells people you are not set up yet. Fix it today. The simplest fix is a new free Gmail address made from your business name, such as `tanyastablecatering@`, or your name plus your trade. Keep it short, easy to say out loud, and free of birth years and nicknames.

If you own a web domain, you can go one step further and have an address at your own business name. That is a nice upgrade, not a requirement. Whatever you choose, use that one address for all business from now on, and set your old address to forward mail so you miss nothing.

### Upgrade two: the signature

A signature is the block of text that appears at the bottom of every email automatically. You set it once in your email settings. It needs four things and no more: name, business, phone, one link.

> Tanya Brooks
> Tanya's Table Catering
> (555) 010-0142
> [your home base link]

Skip the inspirational quotes, the five social icons, and the big image. Four clean lines look professional and work on every phone.

### Upgrade three: the writing

Busy people read the subject line and the first two lines, then decide. Write for that.

1. **Put the point in the subject line.** Not "Hello" or "Question." Write "Quote for March 14 lunch, 60 guests."
2. **Put the ask in the first two lines.** Say what you need before you explain why.
3. **One topic per email.** Two topics means one gets forgotten. Send two emails.
4. **End with a clear next step.** Who does what, by when.

Compare these.

Weak: Subject "Hey." Body: "Hope you're doing well! So I was thinking about the event and there's a few things, also did you get my invoice from last time? Let me know."

Strong: Subject "Need final headcount by Friday for March 14 lunch." Body: "Hi Mr. Ellis, can you send me the final headcount by Friday at noon? I place the food order that afternoon. Right now I am planning for 60. Thank you, Tanya."

### The hard ones: save these as templates

A template is a message you write once and reuse. Write these three while you are calm, so you never have to write them while you are upset.

**The unhappy-customer reply.** Three moves: acknowledge, own it, offer the fix. Never argue in writing. A written argument can be forwarded, posted, and read by strangers forever.

> Subject: About your order on Saturday
>
> Hi Ms. Greene,
>
> Thank you for telling me. You expected the food at 11:30 and it arrived at 12:10, and I understand how stressful that was with guests waiting.
>
> That is on us. We did not leave enough time for traffic.
>
> I would like to make it right. I can refund the delivery fee today and take 15 percent off your next order. May I call you this afternoon to talk it through?
>
> Tanya Brooks

**The quote that protects your scope.** Scope means exactly what is included in the job. Most money fights start because the scope was never written down. A good quote lists what is included, what is not, the price, and how long the price is good.

> Subject: Quote for March 14 lunch, 60 guests
>
> Hi Mr. Ellis,
>
> Here is the quote for your March 14 lunch.
>
> Included: buffet lunch for 60 (two entrees, two sides, salad, rolls, tea and lemonade), delivery, setup, serving pieces, and disposable plates and utensils.
>
> Not included: staff to serve during the meal, rentals such as tables and linens, and cleanup after the event. I am happy to price any of these.
>
> Total: [amount]. A 50 percent deposit holds the date, and the balance is due the day of the event.
>
> This quote is good for 14 days. Anything added after the deposit will be priced separately and confirmed by email before we do it.
>
> To book, reply "approved" and I will send the invoice.

**The polite no.** You will get requests that are the wrong fit, the wrong date, or the wrong price. Say no quickly and kindly, with no long excuse.

> Hi Jordan,
>
> Thank you for thinking of us. I am not able to take this one on, since we are fully booked that weekend. If your date is flexible, I have openings the following two Saturdays. Otherwise, I hope the event goes beautifully.
>
> Tanya

## Do this now

1. Create or confirm your professional business address.
2. Open your email settings and build your four-line signature.
3. Send a test email to a neighbor in the session. Check that the signature shows and the link works.
4. Rewrite one real email you need to send this week using the four writing rules.
5. Draft your own versions of the three hard emails using the examples.
6. Save all three where you can find them: as drafts, in a document, or as templates in Gmail.

Finished result: a professional address, a working signature, and three ready-to-use templates saved.

## Common mistakes

- **Keeping the old nickname address "for now."** Fix: make the new one today and forward the old one to it.
- **Subject lines that say nothing.** Fix: put the point and the date in the subject.
- **Burying the ask at the bottom.** Fix: ask in the first two lines.
- **Defending yourself to an upset customer.** Fix: acknowledge, own it, offer the fix, then move to a phone call.
- **Quotes with only a price.** Fix: always list what is included and what is not.

## Check yourself

- Would you be comfortable reading your email address out loud to a bank officer?
- What four things are in your signature?
- What are the three moves in the unhappy-customer reply?
- Does your quote say what is not included?

## What you walk away with

A business email address, a clean signature, four rules for clear writing, and saved templates for the unhappy customer, the quote, and the polite no.
