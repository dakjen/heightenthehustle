# Four AI Uses That Pay Off in a Small Business

> Put AI on the four jobs with the highest payoff. It does the blank-page 80%, and you do the final 20%.

## Why this matters

A tool is only worth your time if it takes work off your plate. AI can do a thousand things, and most of them will not matter to your business. A few matter a lot. The owner of a catering company does not need AI to write poems. She needs a faster way to answer inquiry emails, a clear list of questions when deciding whether to rent a commercial kitchen, and a quick read on the fine print in a vendor contract. Knowing where AI pays off keeps an hour with it from turning into an hour with nothing to show.

## What you'll be able to do

- Put AI on the four uses that return the most time in a small business
- Write a working prompt for each one
- Apply the 80/20 split and protect the part that is yours
- Use AI to pressure-test a real decision before you commit money to it

## The lesson

### The 80/20 split

The slowest part of most writing is the blank page. Once something is on the screen, fixing it goes quickly. That is where AI earns its keep. AI does the blank-page 80%, the structure and the opening set of words, in seconds. You do the final 20%, which is where your facts, your judgment and your voice live. That 20% is what makes the work true and makes it yours, so it cannot be skipped. Hold that split in mind for every use below.

### Use 1: Drafts of writing you already have to do

Start with the writing already on your list, including the writing you keep putting off. Client emails, service descriptions and social posts are the big three. You are not adding work. You are speeding up work that is already there.

> I run a lawn care business. A customer emailed asking why her bill went up $15 this month. The reason is that we added edging along her new walkway, which she asked for in person. Write a friendly, clear reply. Under 100 words.

A good answer thanks her for asking, explains the edging in one sentence, and offers to remove the service if she prefers. Your 20% is confirming the amount, making sure the tone fits this customer, and adding your name.

### Use 2: Thinking through decisions

AI is useful as a thinking partner. It does not know the right answer for your business, and it should not make the choice. What it can do is lay out the pros, the cons and the questions you should be asking. The questions are often the most valuable part, because they show you what you have not yet priced in.

> I own a one-person mobile detailing business making about $4,000 a month. I am thinking about hiring a part-time helper for 15 hours a week. Give me the pros, the cons, and the questions I should be asking before I decide.

A good answer lists a handful of real pros (more jobs per day, less burnout), real cons (payroll cost, training time, responsibility for someone else's work), and questions such as: Do you have enough steady demand to fill those hours? What does it cost to have an employee beyond the hourly wage? What happens if a helper damages a car? Treat any dollar figures or legal rules it mentions as things to verify, and take the final call yourself or with an advisor you trust.

### Use 3: Getting contract and policy language into plain English

Leases, insurance policies, loan offers and government forms are written in language that slows everyone down. AI is good at translating it.

> Explain this in plain English for a business owner: "Tenant shall be responsible for all common area maintenance charges on a pro rata basis."

A good answer tells you that you pay a share of the cost of keeping up shared spaces, like the parking lot and hallways, and that your share is based on how much of the building you rent. Now you know what to ask the landlord: how much is that each month? Use this to understand what you are reading and to walk into the conversation with sharper questions. For anything you are about to sign, a real professional still needs to look at it.

### Use 4: Summarizing anything long

When you face twelve pages of grant guidelines or a long email thread, paste the text in and ask for the short version. Name the exact items you need so the summary answers your questions and not its own.

> Summarize this grant notice in plain English. Tell me who is eligible, how much money is available, the deadline, and what documents I would need to apply.

A good answer gives you those four things in a short list. Then go back to the original and confirm the deadline and the eligibility rules yourself, because a summary can miss or mangle a detail. Before you paste any document, remove private information such as account numbers and customer details. The next lesson covers those safety rules in full.

### Make one use routine

Pick one of the four uses and make it a habit before you add another. A practical trigger: the next time you sit down to write something for the business, open your AI tool, describe what you need, and edit what comes back.

## Do this now

1. Choose a real decision you are weighing in your business. Examples: raising your prices, hiring help, renting a space, adding a service, dropping a difficult client.
2. Open your AI tool and describe the decision in three or four sentences. Include what your business is, the choice you are facing, and what makes it hard. Leave out private details like account numbers and customer names.
3. End your message with: "Give me the pros, the cons, and the questions I haven't thought of."
4. Read the answer. Circle or copy the two or three questions that hit home.
5. Follow up on one of them, for example: "Tell me more about question 3. What would I need to find out?"
6. Write down, in your own words, the one or two things you now need to find out before deciding.

When you finish, you have a pros-and-cons list for a real decision and a short list of the questions you need to answer before you make it.

## Where owners lose time or money

- **Letting AI make the decision.** It does not know your finances, your customers or your life. Fix: use it to surface pros, cons and questions, then decide yourself.
- **Skipping your 20%.** An unedited draft has no real facts and no voice, and customers notice. Fix: check every fact and rewrite until it sounds like you.
- **Acting on a summary without opening the original.** Summaries can drop or change key details, and a missed deadline costs the whole grant. Fix: confirm deadlines, dollar amounts and requirements in the source document.
- **Treating a plain-English explanation as legal advice.** Knowing what a clause means is not the same as knowing whether it is fair. Fix: have a professional review anything you will sign.
- **Trying every use at once.** You end up dabbling and saving no time. Fix: pick one use, make it routine, then add the next.

## Before you move on

- Which of the four uses will save you the most time this month?
- For the decision you worked on today, which open question could change your answer, and who can settle it?
- For the last summary you relied on, did you confirm the deadline and dollar amounts in the original?
- What in your business belongs in your 20%: which facts, prices and promises only you can confirm?

## What you walk away with

Four dependable ways to put AI to work in your business, and a clear set of pros, cons and open questions for one real decision you are facing.
