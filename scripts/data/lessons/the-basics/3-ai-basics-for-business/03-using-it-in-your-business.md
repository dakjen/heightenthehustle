# Using It in Your Business

> The four highest-payoff uses of AI in a small business. AI does the blank-page 80%, and you do the final 20%.

## Why this matters

A new tool is only worth your time if it takes work off your plate. There are a thousand things AI can do, and most of them will not matter to your business. A few of them matter a lot. The owner of a catering company does not need AI to write poems. She needs a faster way to answer inquiry emails, a clear head when deciding whether to rent a commercial kitchen, and help understanding the fine print in a vendor contract. Knowing where AI pays off keeps you from playing with it for an hour and getting nothing done.

## What you'll learn

- The four uses of AI that pay off most in a small business
- How to write prompts for each one, with full examples
- What the 80/20 split means and which part is yours
- How to use AI to think through a real decision

## The lesson

### The 80/20 split

The hardest part of most writing is the blank page. Once something is on the screen, fixing it is easy. That is where AI earns its keep. AI does the blank-page 80%, the structure and the first set of words, in seconds. You do the final 20%, which is where your facts, your judgment, and your voice live. That 20% is the part that makes the work true and makes it yours, so it cannot be skipped. Keep that split in mind for every use below.

### Use 1: First drafts of writing you already have to do

Start with the writing you are already doing, or avoiding. Client emails, service descriptions, and social posts are the big three. You are not adding new work. You are speeding up work that is already on your list.

> I run a lawn care business. A customer emailed asking why her bill went up $15 this month. The reason is that we added edging along her new walkway, which she asked for in person. Write a friendly, clear reply. Under 100 words.

A good answer thanks her for asking, explains the edging in one sentence, and offers to remove the service if she prefers. Your 20% is confirming the amount, making sure the tone fits this customer, and adding your name.

### Use 2: Thinking through decisions

AI is useful as a thinking partner. It will not know the right answer for your business, and it should not make the choice. What it can do is lay out the pros, the cons, and the questions you should be asking. The questions are often the most valuable part, because they show you what you have not considered.

> I own a one-person mobile detailing business making about $4,000 a month. I am thinking about hiring a part-time helper for 15 hours a week. Give me the pros, the cons, and the questions I should be asking before I decide.

A good answer lists a handful of real pros (more jobs per day, less burnout), real cons (payroll cost, training time, responsibility for someone else's work), and questions such as: Do you have enough steady demand to fill those hours? What does it cost to have an employee beyond the hourly wage? What happens if a helper damages a car? Treat any dollar figures or legal rules it mentions as things to verify, and take the final call yourself or with an advisor you trust.

### Use 3: Explaining jargon in plain English

Business is full of language that seems designed to confuse. Leases, insurance policies, loan offers, and government forms all have it. AI is good at translating.

> Explain this in plain English, like I am new to business: "Tenant shall be responsible for all common area maintenance charges on a pro rata basis."

A good answer tells you that you pay a share of the cost of keeping up shared spaces, like the parking lot and hallways, and that your share is based on how much of the building you rent. Now you know what to ask the landlord: how much is that each month? Use this to understand what you are reading. For anything you are about to sign, a real professional still needs to look at it.

### Use 4: Summarizing anything long

When you face twelve pages of grant guidelines or a long email thread, paste the text in and ask for the short version.

> Summarize this grant notice in plain English. Tell me who is eligible, how much money is available, the deadline, and what documents I would need to apply.

A good answer gives you those four things in a short list. Then go back to the original and confirm the deadline and the eligibility rules yourself, because a summary can miss or mangle a detail. Before you paste any document, remove private information such as account numbers and customer details. The next lesson covers those safety rules in full.

### Putting it to work

Pick one of the four uses and make it a habit before you add another. The simplest starting point is this: the next time you sit down to write something for your business and feel stuck, open your AI tool first, describe what you need, and edit what comes back.

## Do this now

1. Think of a real decision you are weighing in your business. Examples: raising your prices, hiring help, renting a space, adding a service, dropping a difficult client.
2. Open your AI tool and describe the decision in three or four sentences. Include what your business is, the choice you are facing, and anything that makes it hard. Leave out private details like account numbers and customer names.
3. End your message with: "Give me the pros, the cons, and the questions I haven't thought of."
4. Read the answer. Circle or copy the two or three questions that hit home.
5. Follow up on one of them, for example: "Tell me more about question 3. What would I need to find out?"
6. Write down, in your own words, the one or two things you now need to learn before deciding.

When you finish, you have a pros-and-cons list for a real decision and a short list of the questions you need to answer before you make it.

## Common mistakes

- **Letting AI make the decision.** It does not know your finances, your customers, or your life. Fix: use it to surface pros, cons, and questions, then decide yourself.
- **Skipping your 20%.** An unedited draft has no real facts and no voice. Fix: check every fact and rewrite until it sounds like you.
- **Trusting a summary without looking at the original.** Summaries can drop or change key details. Fix: confirm deadlines, dollar amounts, and requirements in the source document.
- **Treating a plain-English explanation as legal advice.** Understanding a clause is not the same as knowing if it is fair. Fix: have a professional review anything you will sign.
- **Trying every use at once.** You end up dabbling and saving no time. Fix: pick one use, make it routine, then add the next.
