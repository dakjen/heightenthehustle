# Using It Safely

> Four rules that keep your customers, your money, and your name protected when you use AI.

## Why this matters

Your business runs on trust. Customers give you their addresses, their phone numbers, sometimes the keys to their homes. Funders and agencies take what you write in an application as true. One careless moment with an AI tool can damage that trust. Imagine a home health aide who pastes a client's full name, address, and medical needs into a chat tool to get help writing a care note. Or a contractor who copies an AI answer about permit rules into a bid, and the rule turns out to be wrong. Neither person meant harm. Both now have a problem. Four simple rules prevent almost all of it.

## What you'll learn

- What never to type or paste into an AI tool
- Why and how to verify anything that matters
- Why you read every word before it goes out under your name
- How to keep unchecked AI "facts" out of applications and client documents

## The lesson

### Rule 1: Never paste in what you wouldn't put on a postcard

A postcard can be read by anyone who handles it. Treat an AI chat the same way. What you type is sent to the company that runs the tool and stored on its computers. Depending on the tool and your settings, it may be kept, reviewed, or used to improve the product. Policies differ and they change, so the safe habit is to never put private information in to begin with.

That means:

- **No customer data.** No full names with addresses, phone numbers, emails, health details, or payment information.
- **No banking info.** No account numbers, routing numbers, card numbers, or tax ID and Social Security numbers.
- **No passwords.** Not yours, not anyone's, not ever.

You can still get the help you need. Swap the private details for placeholders. Here is the unsafe version of a prompt:

> Write a late payment reminder to Denise Carter at 4410 Elm Ave, 410-555-0199, who owes $380 on card ending 4421.

Here is the safe version:

> Write a polite late payment reminder to a client named [NAME] who owes [AMOUNT] for a cleaning service from three weeks ago. Friendly but firm. Under 80 words.

You get the same quality draft. Then you fill in the real name and amount yourself, outside the AI tool.

### Rule 2: Verify anything that matters against a real source

AI can be confidently wrong. It can state a wrong tax deadline, a license rule from a different state, or a statistic it made up, and it will sound just as sure as when it is right. So any answer that affects your money, your legal standing, or your customers gets checked against a real source before you act on it.

A real source is the place the information comes from. For a license rule, that is the city or state agency that issues the license. For a tax question, that is the tax agency or your accountant. For a contract question, that is a lawyer. For a product, that is the manufacturer's label or instructions. AI is not your lawyer or accountant. It can help you understand a topic and prepare good questions, and then a real person or official source gives you the answer you rely on.

If the tool shows you a source for its claim, go look at that source yourself. Sometimes the source does not say what the tool claimed, and sometimes it does not exist.

### Rule 3: Never send AI-written text you haven't read

Whatever you send goes out under your name. The customer does not know or care that a tool drafted it. If the message promises a discount you do not offer, gives the wrong date, or sounds cold, that is you saying it. AI drafts can include invented details, placeholder text like "[insert date]," or a promise you never made.

Before anything leaves your hands, read every word. Check names, dates, prices, and promises. Read it out loud once to catch anything that does not sound like you. This takes a minute or two, and it protects years of reputation.

### Rule 4: Never pass along unchecked AI "facts" in applications or client documents

Applications and client documents are held to a higher standard, because other people make decisions based on them. A grant reviewer, a lender, or a customer signing your agreement assumes what is written is true. If an AI tool adds a number to your grant application, such as how many businesses like yours exist in your city, and that number is invented, you have submitted false information, even if you did not know it.

So for these documents, every fact must be one you can back up. Your own numbers, like your revenue, your years in business, and your customer count, come from your own records. Any outside fact gets verified and you keep a note of where it came from. If you cannot verify it, take it out. It is fine for AI to help with the wording. The facts have to be yours.

### A quick check before you hit send

Ask yourself four questions each time:

1. Did I keep private information out of the tool?
2. Did I verify the facts that matter?
3. Did I read every word?
4. Could I back up every claim if someone asked?

## Do this now

1. Think of a factual question about your industry where the answer matters. Examples: "What license does a home daycare need in my state?" or "How long should cooked food be kept hot at a catered event?"
2. Ask your AI tool the question. Include your state or city if it applies.
3. Write down the answer it gives, including any specific numbers, names, or rules.
4. Now check it against a real source: the agency that makes the rule, an official publication, or a licensed professional you know.
5. Compare the two. Note what the AI got right, what it got wrong, and what it left out.
6. Write one sentence about what you learned from the comparison.

When you finish, you have a side-by-side comparison of an AI answer and a real source, and firsthand proof of why you verify.

## Common mistakes

- **Pasting a whole customer list or invoice into the chat.** That hands private data to a third party. Fix: remove or replace names, contact details, and payment info before you paste anything.
- **Assuming a detailed answer must be correct.** Specific numbers and official-sounding names can be invented. Fix: confirm with the agency, the professional, or the original document.
- **Copying and sending without reading.** Wrong details and leftover placeholders go out under your name. Fix: read every word, out loud if you can.
- **Letting AI add statistics to a grant or loan application.** You are responsible for every claim. Fix: use only facts you can trace to your records or a real source.
- **Using AI in place of a lawyer or accountant.** It can explain, but it cannot take responsibility. Fix: use it to prepare your questions, then ask the professional.

## Check yourself

- What three kinds of information never go into an AI tool?
- What counts as a "real source" for a license question? For a tax question?
- Why does it matter that AI-written text goes out under your name?
- What do you do with an outside fact in an application if you cannot verify it?

## What you walk away with

Four rules you can apply every time you use AI: keep private data out, verify what matters, read before you send, and keep unchecked facts out of anything official.
