# How to Ask for What You Need

> Give AI context, a specific task, and a format, then refine the first draft until it sounds like you.

## Why this matters

The quality of what AI gives you depends on what you give it. Type "write an ad" and you get something bland that could be for any business in any city. Tell it who you are, what you want, and how you want it, and you get something you can use in a few minutes. For a business owner, that is the difference between a tool that wastes your time and one that saves it. A cleaning business owner who learns to ask well can turn out a week of client texts, a reply to a tough review, and a new service description in one sitting.

## What you'll learn

- How to talk to AI like a smart new employee on day one
- The three parts of a good request: context, task, and format
- How to refine a first draft with follow-up messages
- How to edit AI writing until it sounds like you

## The lesson

### A smart new employee on day one

What you type to an AI tool is called a prompt. The best way to write one is to imagine a smart new employee on their first day. They are capable and willing, and they know nothing about your business. If you told that person "write something for our customers," they would have to guess at everything. If you told them who your customers are, what the message is for, and how long it should be, they would do well. AI works the same way. It cannot read your mind, and it will fill any gap you leave with a guess.

### The three parts of a good request

Every strong prompt has three parts.

**Context.** Who you are and who this is for. "I run a cleaning business in Baltimore."

**A specific task.** Exactly what you want made. "Write a short, friendly text offering past clients 15% off a spring deep-clean."

**A format.** The shape of the answer: how long, how many versions, what style. "Under 50 words, three options."

Put together, the prompt reads:

> I run a cleaning business in Baltimore. Write a short, friendly text offering past clients 15% off a spring deep-clean. Under 50 words, three options.

Compare that to the weak version: "Write a text about a sale." The weak prompt gets you a message with a made-up discount, no mention of cleaning, and a pile of exclamation points. The strong prompt gets you three short options that name your real offer, something like: "Hi! It's Tanya from Johnson Cleaning. Spring is here, and past clients get 15% off a deep-clean this month. Want me to save you a spot?" A good answer uses your details, stays inside your word limit, and sounds like something a real person would send.

### More examples

The same three parts work for any kind of business.

> I own a two-chair barbershop. A customer left a two-star review saying he waited 40 minutes past his appointment time. Write a calm, professional public reply that apologizes, does not make excuses, and invites him back. Under 80 words.

> I run a home daycare for children ages 1 to 4. Write a welcome note for new parents that explains drop-off time is 7:30 to 8:30 a.m. and asks them to pack a change of clothes. Warm tone, one short paragraph.

Notice that the real facts, like the wait time and the drop-off hours, come from you. AI supplies the wording. If you leave a fact out, it may invent one.

### The first answer is a first draft

Treat the first answer as a first draft. You are in a conversation, and the tool remembers what was said earlier in the same chat. So do not start over. Reply, refine, redirect. The back-and-forth is free. Useful follow-ups include:

- "Shorter. Cut it in half."
- "Too formal. Make it sound like a text from a neighbor."
- "I like option 2. Give me three more like that one."
- "Take out the exclamation points and the word 'thrilled.'"
- "Add that the offer ends April 30."

Three or four rounds of this is normal. Each round takes seconds, and each one moves the draft closer to what you want.

### Make it sound like you

AI writing has habits. It tends to be a little too polished, a little too excited, and fond of words you would never say out loud. Your customers know your voice, and a message that does not sound like you can feel cold or fake. So the last step is always yours. Read the draft out loud. Change any word you would not use. Check every fact, name, price, and date. Add the personal touch only you know, like asking how the new puppy is doing. You can also help the tool match your style by pasting in a message you wrote yourself and saying, "Match the tone of this."

## Do this now

1. Pick one real client you have a reason to text this week, such as a reminder, a thank-you, a special offer, or a check-in.
2. Open your AI tool and write a prompt with all three parts: context about your business, the specific task, and the format you want.
3. Leave out the client's full name, phone number, address, and any private details. Use a first name or "a past client."
4. Read the answer and send at least two follow-up messages to refine it.
5. Copy the best version and edit it by hand until it sounds like you. Read it out loud and check the facts.
6. Send the text to your client.

When you finish, you have sent a real message to a real client, drafted with AI and finished in your own voice.

## Common mistakes

- **One-line prompts with no details.** Vague in, vague out. Fix: always include context, task, and format.
- **Starting a brand-new chat when the answer misses.** You lose everything the tool already knows. Fix: reply in the same chat and tell it what to change.
- **Sending the first draft as is.** It will sound like a robot and may contain wrong details. Fix: refine, then edit by hand and read it out loud.
- **Leaving out your real facts.** The tool will make up prices, dates, or offers to fill the gap. Fix: give it the real numbers and check them in the result.
- **Not asking for a length.** You get five paragraphs when you needed two sentences. Fix: say "under 50 words" or "one short paragraph."
