# Send and Organize Email

> Write emails that get read and answered, then keep your inbox under control with archive, stars, labels, and filters.

## Why this matters

Email is where a lot of business money moves. Quotes go out, invoices get sent, customers ask questions, and funders send deadlines. If your emails are hard to understand, people put them off and you wait days for an answer. If your inbox is a pile, you lose the message you need at the moment you need it. Think of a contractor who sends a quote with the subject "hey" and no price until the fourth paragraph. The customer never opens it, and the job goes to someone else. Clear emails and a tidy inbox get you answered faster and paid faster.

## What you'll learn

- The three parts of a business email that gets read and answered
- How to write a subject line that says what the email is about
- How to use archive, stars, and labels to keep your inbox clear
- How to set a filter so Gmail files recurring senders for you

## The lesson

### The job of a business email

A business email has one job: get read and get answered. People are busy, and most of them look at email on a phone, where they see a subject line and a line or two of text. So you build every email from three parts.

1. **A subject line that says what it's about.** The reader should know the topic before opening it.
2. **The point in the first two lines.** Say what you need or what you are telling them right away. Background comes after.
3. **A clear next step at the end.** Tell the reader exactly what you want them to do, and by when if it matters.

### Subject lines

A good subject line is specific. It names the thing and often the date or the action. Compare these:

| Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- |
| Hey | Quote for kitchen deep-clean, 123 Oak St |
| Question | Can you confirm Saturday 10am delivery? |
| Invoice | Invoice 004 for March catering, due April 15 |

A strong subject line also helps you later. When you search your inbox in six months, "Invoice 004 for March catering" is easy to find. "Hey" is not.

### A full example

Here is a weak email:

> Subject: following up
>
> Hi, hope you are doing well. I wanted to reach out because we talked a while back at the event and I have been thinking about what you said and I was wondering if maybe you still needed someone.

The reader does not know who this is, what it is about, or what to do. Here is the same message done right:

> Subject: Cleaning quote for your Charles St office
>
> Hi Ms. Reed,
>
> I can clean your Charles St office twice a week for $240 a month. That covers floors, restrooms, trash, and the break room.
>
> We met at the March small business mixer, and you mentioned your current service was ending.
>
> If that price works, reply with a start date and I will send a short agreement to sign. I have openings beginning May 1.
>
> Tanya Johnson
> Johnson Cleaning Co.
> (410) 555-0142

The subject names the topic. The price and the offer are in the first two lines. The last paragraph tells her exactly what to do next. Before you send any email, read it once out loud and check the name, the numbers, and the date.

### Three tools for a clear inbox

Your inbox should hold only what still needs something from you. Three Gmail tools make that possible.

**Archive what's done.** Archive removes an email from your inbox without deleting it. It stays in your account, and you can find it any time with the search bar or under All Mail. Look for the archive button (a box with a down arrow) when a message is open or selected. Once you have answered something or no longer need to act on it, archive it.

**Star what needs action.** A star is a marker you put on an email that needs you to do something: reply, pay, send a document. Click the star next to the message. Click "Starred" in the left menu and you have your to-do list. Remove the star when the task is done.

**Label by category.** A label is like a folder name you attach to an email. Start with three: Clients, Money, and Receipts. To create one, look in the left menu for the option to create a new label, or open a message and use the label button at the top. One email can carry more than one label, and labeled messages stay findable after you archive them.

### Filters: an inbox that files itself

A filter is a rule you give Gmail, such as "every email from this sender gets this label." It is perfect for recurring senders, like your payment app's receipts, your bank's statements, or a supplier's invoices.

1. Open an email from the sender you want to file automatically.
2. Open the "more" menu on the message (three dots) and choose the option to filter messages like this. You can also find filters under Settings (the gear icon), in the section for filters and blocked addresses.
3. Confirm the sender's address is filled in, then continue to create the filter.
4. Check the box to apply a label and choose one, such as Receipts.
5. If you do not need to see these as they arrive, also check the box to skip the inbox. Leave it unchecked for anything you need to act on.
6. Create the filter. You can also apply it to the matching emails you already have.

## Do this now

1. In your business Gmail, create three labels: Clients, Money, and Receipts.
2. Go through your inbox. Archive everything that is done. Star everything that still needs action from you.
3. Add a label to at least five emails.
4. Pick one recurring sender, such as a payment app or supplier, and create a filter that labels its emails automatically.
5. Write one real business email you need to send this week. Give it a specific subject line, put the point in the first two lines, and end with a clear next step.
6. Read it out loud, check the facts, and send it.

When you finish, you have a labeled inbox with a short starred to-do list, one working filter, and one real email sent.

## Common mistakes

- **Vague subject lines like "Hi" or "Question."** They get skipped and cannot be searched later. Fix: name the thing, and add the date or action.
- **Burying the point under a long greeting.** Readers quit before they reach it. Fix: put what you need in the first two lines, then give background.
- **Ending with no next step.** "Let me know" leaves the reader guessing. Fix: ask for one specific thing, like "Reply with a start date."
- **Deleting instead of archiving.** A deleted receipt or agreement is gone when you need it for taxes or a dispute. Fix: archive business email. Delete only true junk.
- **Making twenty labels on day one.** Too many choices and you stop filing. Fix: start with Clients, Money, and Receipts, and add a label only when you keep needing it.

- Where do you look to see every email that still needs action from you?
- Which sender in your inbox would be worth a filter, and what label would it get?

A way to write emails that get answered, and an inbox system of archive, stars, labels, and filters that shows you only what still needs your attention.
