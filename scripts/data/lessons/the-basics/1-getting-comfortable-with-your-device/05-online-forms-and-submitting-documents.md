# Online Forms and Submitting Documents

> Fill out an online application, upload the right document, and confirm your submission actually went through.

## Why this matters

Grants, loans, vendor registrations, licenses, permits, and market booth sign-ups are almost all handled through online forms now. The form is often the only thing standing between you and the opportunity. Imagine a food vendor applying for a spot at a weekend market. She fills in every box, but never clicks the final Submit button, and she never notices because she did not look for a confirmation. The deadline passes and her spot goes to someone else. Knowing how forms work, from the first box to the confirmation message, means your effort actually counts.

## What you'll learn

- How online applications are built and how to move through them
- What the common form parts are called and how to use each one
- How to upload a document and check that it attached
- How to confirm a submission was successful and keep proof

## The lesson

### How online applications work

An online form is a paper form on a screen. Each blank space is called a **field**. You click or tap a field, type your answer, and move to the next one. On a computer, pressing the **Tab** key jumps to the next field, which is faster than clicking each one.

Fields marked with a star (*) or the word "required" must be filled in. If you skip one, the form will not let you finish. Longer applications are split into several pages with a **Next** or **Continue** button at the bottom of each. Some applications ask you to create an account first so you can save your progress and come back later. If you see a **Save** or **Save draft** button, use it often.

### The parts of a form

Forms use a small set of building blocks. Once you know them, every form looks familiar.

- **Text box.** A blank space where you type, such as your name or business address.
- **Dropdown menu.** A box with a small arrow. Click it to see a list, then click one choice. States and business types are often dropdowns.
- **Radio buttons.** Small circles. You can pick only one, as in a Yes or No question.
- **Checkboxes.** Small squares. You can check as many as apply.
- **Date picker.** A small calendar that opens when you click a date field.
- **Upload button.** A button that says something like **Choose file**, **Browse**, **Attach**, or **Upload**.

Type answers exactly the way the form asks. If a phone field shows an example with dashes, use dashes. If it asks for your legal business name, type it the way it appears on your registration, not your nickname for it.

### Get ready before you start

Many forms **time out**, which means they close and erase your answers if you leave them sitting too long. The fix is to gather everything first. Read the instructions or the checklist at the top of the application. Collect the information you will need, such as your business name, address, phone, email, and start date. Then find the documents the form asks for, and check that each one is the right file type and clearly named. For long written answers, type them in a document first and then copy and paste them into the form. That way a time-out costs you nothing.

### Uploading a document

To **upload** means to send a copy of a file from your device to a website. It is the opposite of a download. An **attachment** is the same idea in an email: a file that travels along with your message. Both depend on you knowing where the file is, which is why your business folder matters.

1. Click the upload button on the form.
2. A window opens showing your files. Go to your business folder.
3. Click the file you want, then click **Open** or **Choose**. On a phone, tap the file.
4. Look for the file name to appear next to the upload button. That is your sign it attached.
5. If the form rejects the file, read the message. It is usually the wrong file type or a file that is too large. Most forms list what they accept, and PDF is the most common request.

### Submitting and confirming

Filling in the form is not the same as submitting it. Nothing is sent until you click the final button, usually labeled **Submit**, **Send**, or **Finish**. Before you click it, review your answers. Many applications show a summary page for this purpose. Check your email address and phone number twice, because those are how the office will reach you.

After you click Submit, the form may show errors instead of finishing. These usually appear in red near the field that needs fixing, or in a list at the top. Fix each one and submit again.

You know the submission worked when you see a **confirmation page**, often with the words "Thank you" or "Your application has been received," and sometimes a confirmation number. Many systems also send a confirmation email. Do three things before you close the page: write down any confirmation number, take a **screenshot** (a picture of your screen) or save the page, and check your email for the confirmation message, including the spam or junk folder. If you get neither a confirmation page nor an email, do not assume it went through. Contact the office and ask.

## Try it

1. Open the practice business application in this lesson. It uses fictional information and goes nowhere outside this course.
2. Before typing anything, read the whole form and note which fields are required.
3. Fill in each field using the practice business details on the reference sheet in this lesson's downloads. Use the Tab key to move between fields at least five times.
4. At the upload step, attach the sample document you saved in your business folder in the Files lesson. Confirm that its name appears on the form.
5. Leave one required field blank on purpose and click Submit. Find the error message, read it, and fix the field.
6. Review everything and submit again.
7. Take a screenshot of the confirmation page and save it in your business folder with a clear name. You now have a completed application and saved proof that it was received.

## Common mistakes

- **Closing the page before clicking Submit.** Scroll all the way to the bottom and look for the final button.
- **Assuming it went through.** Look for a confirmation page or email, and save proof.
- **Uploading the wrong file or the wrong type.** Check the file name on the form after you attach it, and convert to PDF when asked.
- **Typing long answers straight into the form.** Write them in a document first, then paste them in.
- **Mistyping your own email address.** Read it one character at a time before you submit.
