# Getting Connected and Navigating the Internet

> Connect to Wi-Fi, fix basic connection problems, use browser tabs and bookmarks, and tell a search engine from a web address.

## Why this matters

Licenses, permits, tax accounts, supplier catalogs, and loan applications have nearly all moved online. If you can get connected and reach the right website, you can handle business from your shop, your van, or your kitchen table. Think of a home repair contractor who needs to renew a license between jobs. If he knows how to join a trusted network, type the licensing office's address, and save the page for next year, the renewal takes fifteen minutes. If he ends up on a look-alike site from a search result, he may pay a middleman an extra fee for something the real office does directly.

## What you'll learn

- How to connect to a Wi-Fi network and tell if you are connected
- What to check first when the internet stops working
- How to use browser tabs and bookmarks
- The difference between typing a web address and using a search engine
- How to download a form from a website

## The lesson

### Connecting to Wi-Fi

**Wi-Fi** is a wireless connection between your device and a small box called a **router**, which is connected to the internet. Each Wi-Fi network has a name, and most are protected by a password. The Wi-Fi symbol looks like a small fan of curved lines. When it shows in the corner of your screen with the lines filled in, you are connected.

1. Open **Settings** and choose **Wi-Fi** or **Network and Internet**. On a computer, you can also click the Wi-Fi symbol in the corner of the screen.
2. Make sure Wi-Fi is turned on.
3. Choose the network name from the list. Pick only networks you know and trust, such as your home, your shop, or the classroom network.
4. Type the password exactly. Capital and lowercase letters count.
5. Wait for the word **Connected** or a check mark next to the network name.

Phones can also reach the internet through **cellular data**, which comes from your phone plan and does not need Wi-Fi. That is why your phone may work in the parking lot while your laptop does not.

### When the connection is not working

Signs of a connection problem include pages that never finish loading, a message such as "No internet" or "You are offline," or a Wi-Fi symbol with an X, a slash, or an exclamation mark. Work through these checks in order, from simplest to biggest.

1. Check that Wi-Fi is on and that **airplane mode** is off. Airplane mode turns off all wireless connections.
2. Check that you are on the right network, not a neighbor's.
3. Turn Wi-Fi off, wait a few seconds, and turn it back on.
4. Move closer to the router. Walls and distance weaken the signal.
5. Restart your device.
6. Unplug the router, wait about thirty seconds, and plug it back in. Give it a few minutes to start.
7. Try another device. If nothing in the building can connect, the problem is with your internet service, and it is time to call your provider.

### The browser, tabs, and bookmarks

A **browser** is the app you use to visit websites. Chrome, Safari, Edge, and Firefox are all browsers, and they all work the same basic way. The long box at the top is the **address bar**. The arrows next to it take you back and forward through pages you just visited, and the circular arrow reloads the page.

A **tab** is one open page inside the browser. Tabs let you keep several pages open at once, like several folders open on a desk. Click the plus sign at the top to open a new tab, click a tab to switch to it, and click the X on a tab to close it. On a phone, look for a small square or a number near the address bar to see your tabs.

A **bookmark** (some browsers say **favorite**) saves a page so you can return with one click. Look for a star or a bookmark shape near the address bar, or open the browser's menu and choose to add a bookmark. Bookmark the pages you use for business, such as your bank, your licensing office, and your suppliers. A bookmark also protects you, because it always takes you to the real site you saved.

### Web address or search engine?

A **web address** (also called a URL) is the exact location of one website, like a street address. It usually ends in something like .com, .org, or .gov. When you type a full address into the address bar and press Enter, you go straight to that site.

A **search engine**, such as Google or Bing, is more like asking for directions. You type words, and it gives you a list of pages that might match. In most browsers the address bar does both jobs: type an address and you go there, type plain words and you get search results. Search is the right tool when you do not know the address. Just read the results carefully. The first few results are often ads, marked with a small word such as "Sponsored" or "Ad," and they are not always the official site. Before you click, look at the address shown under the result's title. Government offices in the United States generally use addresses ending in .gov.

### Downloading a form

Many offices post forms as PDF files. Click the link or the download button, usually marked with a downward arrow. The browser shows the download in a small panel, and the file lands in your Downloads folder. Rename it and move it to your business folder, just as you practiced in the last lesson.

## Try it

1. Open Wi-Fi settings and confirm you are connected to an approved network. If you are in class, use the classroom network.
2. Turn Wi-Fi off, watch the symbol change, and turn it back on.
3. Open your browser and use search to find the official website for business licenses or registration in your city, county, or state.
4. Check the address under the result before you click. Skip anything marked as an ad.
5. Bookmark the page.
6. Open a new tab and find a second resource, such as your local small business development office. Switch between the two tabs.
7. Download the sample form from this lesson's downloads, then move it into your business folder. You now have a working connection, one bookmarked business resource, and a downloaded form filed where you can find it.

## Common mistakes

- **Clicking the first search result without reading it.** Check for the "Sponsored" label and read the address first.
- **Joining any open network that shows up.** Connect only to networks you know and trust.
- **Typing the Wi-Fi password in the wrong case.** Retype it slowly and check capital letters.
- **Opening dozens of tabs.** Close what you are done with and bookmark what you need to keep.
- **Calling for help before the basic checks.** Run the seven checks first. Most problems end at step three or five.
