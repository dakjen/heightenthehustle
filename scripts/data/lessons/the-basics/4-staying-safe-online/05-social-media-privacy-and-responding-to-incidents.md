# Protect Your Business Pages and Recover Fast From a Takeover

> Control what the public can see, keep personal and business separate, shut down impersonators, and get a compromised account back through official channels.

## Why this matters

For many small businesses, a social media page is the storefront. That makes it valuable to you and to criminals. A hair braider's business page gets taken over on a Friday. The thief posts a fake "deposit special" and collects payments from her regular customers all weekend. She loses money, bookings and some of the trust she spent years building. Tight privacy settings lower the odds of a takeover, and a written plan shortens the damage. Your reputation is a business asset, and it deserves the same protection as your cash.

## What you'll be able to do

- Set who can see your posts, contact details and connections
- Use location where it sells, not where it exposes you
- Separate personal and business accounts, with helper access through roles
- Shut down an account impersonating your business
- Recover a compromised account in seven steps

## The lesson

### Privacy settings

Privacy controls sit under **Settings**, usually in a section called **Privacy** or **Account**, and platforms move them from time to time. Many platforms also offer a privacy checkup that walks through the main choices.

For a personal account, review five things: who can see your posts, who can see your phone number and email, who can see your friends or followers list, who can send you messages, and whether your profile shows up in search engines.

Choosing "friends" or "followers you approve" in place of "public" is a sound default. Remove details you do not need to share, such as your full birth date and home address. Criminals use those details to guess passwords, answer security questions, and write convincing messages to the people you know.

### Location sharing

Location shows up in three ways: a tag you add to a post, hidden location details saved inside a photo, and live location sharing in apps. For a business with a storefront, sharing the shop's address is good marketing. Sharing your home address is not. This matters most if you run the business from home, as many childcare providers, bakers and cleaners do.

Posting "At the beach all week!" in real time also tells the public that your home or shop is empty. Post trip photos after you are back. In your phone's Settings, under **Privacy** or **Location**, choose which apps may use your location and turn it off for the camera and for social media apps that do not need it.

### Keeping personal and business separate

Use a business page or business profile for the business, and keep your personal account personal. Most platforms offer a business account type, often with extra tools such as business hours, contact buttons, and insights about your posts. Sign up for business accounts with your business email address and a business phone number where you can, so your personal contact details are not on display.

Separation protects both sides. Customers do not need to see your family photos, your children's school, or your personal opinions, and a problem on one account is less likely to spread to the other.

If the platform lets you add other people to manage a business page, add them through its official roles feature. Do not share your password. Roles let you remove someone's access in one click when they leave. A second trusted person with a role on the page also gives you a way back in if your own account is locked.

### Impersonation

Impersonators copy your name, logo and photos into a fake account, then message your followers. They may announce fake giveaways, ask for deposits, or send links that steal sign-in details. Signs include customers saying they got a strange message from you, a second account with your name and a slightly different spelling, or a recently created account with your photos and very few followers.

When it happens:

1. Do not argue with the fake account.
2. Take screenshots of the profile and its messages.
3. Report the account using the platform's own report option, which usually includes a choice such as "pretending to be someone" or "impersonation."
4. Ask a few customers and friends to report it too.
5. Post a short notice on your real page telling customers which account is yours, and that you will never ask for payment through a direct message from an unfamiliar account.

### If an account is compromised

Signs that someone else is in your account: posts or messages you did not send, a password that suddenly does not work, emails about a changed password or email address that you did not request, or sign-in alerts from places you have never been.

1. **If you can still sign in,** change the password right away to one used nowhere else. Then look in the security settings for an option to sign out of all other devices, and use it.
2. **If you cannot sign in,** use the official recovery option on the platform's own sign-in page, such as "Forgot password" or "My account was hacked." Reach it through the app or by typing the platform's address yourself.
3. Secure the email account connected to it. Change that password too and confirm two-step verification is on.
4. Turn on two-step verification on the recovered account.
5. Check the account settings for anything the intruder changed: the recovery email and phone number, connected apps, page roles, and any payment or advertising details. Remove what you do not recognize.
6. Delete posts and messages the intruder sent, and tell your customers what happened and what to ignore.
7. If a card or bank account was linked, call your bank. Report the incident to the platform, and to the Federal Trade Commission if money was lost.

One caution: people who contact you offering to get your account back for a fee are running a second fraud, and so are most "recovery services" advertised in comment sections. Platforms do not charge to recover an account. Use only the platform's official help pages.

## Try it

1. Sign in to the demonstration account for this course. If you prefer, use your own personal account, and keep your screen private if you do.
2. Open **Settings** and go to the privacy section. Write down the current setting for who can see posts, who can see contact details, and who can send messages.
3. Change at least one setting to be more private.
4. Check whether location is being added to posts, and turn it off where it is not needed.
5. Read the simulated compromise scenario in this lesson's downloads. A fictional bakery owner finds posts she did not write and cannot sign in.
6. Number the response steps on the worksheet in the order you would take them, and write the short notice you would post to customers afterward.
7. Fill in the incident plan on the reference sheet: your key accounts by name only, where each one's recovery option is found, and who your backup page manager will be. You now have tighter privacy settings and a written incident plan ready to use.

## Where owners lose time or money

- **Running the business from a personal profile.** Fix: set up a business page or business account and move customers there.
- **Posting your location in real time.** Fix: share trips and events after they are over, and never tag your home.
- **Sharing your password with someone who helps with posts.** Fix: add them through the platform's roles feature.
- **Paying someone who promises to recover your account.** Fix: use only the official recovery process.
- **Recovering the account but not checking the settings.** Fix: review recovery contacts, connected apps and roles so the intruder cannot come back.

## Before you move on

- Which personal profile details are public right now that could help someone guess a password?
- Does a second trusted person hold a role on your business page?
- If your page were taken over tonight, where is the official recovery option, and what would you tell customers?

## What you walk away with

Tighter privacy settings, a process for impersonation and account recovery through official channels, and a written incident plan.
