# Keep Payments Safe and Catch Fraud Fast

> Verify before money moves, screen payment app, invoice and gift card fraud, set up alerts, and respond fast when a bad charge appears.

## Why this matters

For a small business, one bad payment can be a week's profit or a month's rent. A food truck owner takes a large catering order from a new customer. The customer sends too much through a payment app and asks for the extra back, a request any accommodating owner would honor. She refunds it from her own balance. Days later, the original payment is reversed because it came from a stolen account, and she is out the refund and the food. Money that leaves through a payment app, a wire, or a gift card is very hard to get back. The protection is in the checks that run before you pay, and in catching problems within minutes.

## What you'll be able to do

- Buy online with the strongest dispute rights available
- Shut down the four payment app plays
- Run a three-point check on every invoice before paying
- Treat gift card and verification code requests as fraud every time
- Set up transaction alerts and a five-step fraud response

## The lesson

### Buying online

Buy from sellers you know, or look into an unfamiliar seller before you order. Search for the business name along with words such as "reviews" or "complaints," and look for a real street address, a phone number, and a clear return policy. Be careful with prices far below everyone else's, especially in ads on social media. Fake stores use unbelievable prices to collect card numbers and then ship nothing.

Go to the store by typing its address or using your bookmark, not by clicking a link in a message. The padlock in the address bar means the connection between you and the site is private. It does not mean the business is honest, because fraudulent sites can have a padlock too.

How you pay matters. In the United States, credit cards generally give you the strongest ability to dispute a charge, and a credit card is not connected directly to the cash in your bank account. Debit cards pull straight from your account, so stolen money is your money until the bank sorts it out. Protections vary by card and bank, so ask your bank what applies to your accounts. A business credit card or a separate account used only for online purchases limits how much a thief can reach.

### Payment app fraud

Treat a payment in one of these apps like handing over cash. Once it is sent, it is usually gone, and the app often cannot pull it back. Four scripts are common.

- **The overpayment.** A customer sends too much and asks for a refund of the difference. The original payment later reverses. Do not refund outside the app's own refund feature, and be wary of any overpayment.
- **The accidental payment.** A stranger sends you money "by mistake" and asks you to send it back. Do not send a separate payment. Tell them to contact the app's support, or use the app's official refund option.
- **The fake support call.** Someone claiming to be from the app or your bank says your account is at risk and asks you to move your money or read them a code. Real support staff do not ask for this. Hang up and contact the company through its app.
- **The fake payment screenshot.** A buyer shows you a picture of a "sent" payment. A picture proves nothing. Check your own account balance before you hand over goods.

Send money only to people you know, and confirm the name, phone number, or username before you tap send. For an initial payment to an unfamiliar recipient, a small test amount is a sensible step.

### Invoices and payment requests

Fraudsters send bills for things you never ordered, and they also pose as your real vendors with "updated" bank details. Before paying any invoice, check three things. Did you order this? Does the amount match what you agreed to? Are the payment details the same as last time? If the invoice is unexpected, or the bank account, mailing address, or payment method has changed, call the vendor at a number you already have on file and confirm. If you have staff, set a standing rule that changes to payment details and payments above an amount you choose need a second person's approval.

### Gift cards and verification codes

No legitimate business, bank, court, utility, or government agency demands payment in gift cards. Anyone who asks you to buy gift cards and read them the numbers is committing fraud, every time. A common version targets employees: a text that appears to come from the owner asks a staff member to buy gift cards "for a client" and keep it quiet. Tell your team now that you will never ask for that.

Verification codes work the same way. The code texted to your phone is the key to your account. A buyer who says, "I sent a code to your phone to prove you are real, read it to me," is trying to take over one of your accounts. Never share a code with someone who contacts you.

### Alerts

Transaction alerts are the texts, emails, or app notifications your bank sends when money moves. They let you catch fraud in minutes, not at the end of the month. Open your bank's app or website, go to Settings, and open **Alerts** or **Notifications**. Useful ones include:

- Every purchase, or every purchase over a set amount
- Online purchases
- Withdrawals
- Low balance
- Changes to your password or contact details

Also review your accounts yourself at least once a week.

### If fraud happens

Speed matters more than anything.

1. Call your bank or card company right away using the number on the back of your card or in the official app. Report the charge and ask them to freeze or replace the card.
2. Change the password on the affected account and on your email.
3. Write down what happened: dates, amounts, names, and what was said. Keep screenshots.
4. Report it to the payment app or seller involved, and to the Federal Trade Commission. File a police report if your bank or insurer asks for one.
5. Watch your accounts closely for the next few months.

## Try it

Every scenario in this activity is fictional. Do not open your real banking app in class.

1. Open the payment scenarios in this lesson's downloads. There are six.
2. Sort each one into **pay normally**, **verify first**, or **do not pay**.
3. For each "verify first," write who you would call and where you would get the number.
4. For each "do not pay," write the signal that decided it.
5. Compare your answers with the answer notes on the reference sheet.
6. Fill in the bottom of the sheet: your bank's fraud phone number from the back of your card, and the three alerts you will turn on at home. You now have a decision guide for payments and a fraud contact card ready before you need it.

## Where owners lose time or money

- **Refunding an overpayment with a separate payment.** Fix: use only the app's official refund feature, and wait until funds are truly settled.
- **Accepting a screenshot as proof of payment.** Fix: check your own account before goods leave your hands.
- **Paying a changed invoice without calling.** Fix: confirm changed payment details by phone at a number you already had.
- **Waiting to report fraud.** Fix: call the bank the same hour you notice it.
- **Running business and personal money through one account.** Fix: keep them separate so problems are quicker to spot and limit.

## Before you move on

- Which three alerts are turned on for your business account right now?

A pre-payment check, a list of alerts to turn on, and your bank's fraud number ready before you need it.
