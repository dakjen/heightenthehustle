# Stop Fraud Attempts Before They Cost You

> Screen emails, texts, job offers, QR codes and invoices against a short list of fraud signals, and run one routine every time: stop, verify, report.

## Why this matters

Fraud against small businesses is organized, professional work. The people behind it choose small businesses on purpose: your phone number and email are public, you move fast, and you handle money without a fraud department behind you. Their messages are built to get past sharp, experienced people on a busy day. A cleaning company owner gets an email on a packed Monday that appears to come from her supply vendor: "Our bank details have changed. Please send this month's payment to the new account." The logo is right. The tone is right. If the payment goes out, the money is gone and the real vendor still needs to be paid. The defense is a fixed routine that runs the same way every time, whoever in the business opens the message.

## What you'll be able to do

- Spot the signals that show up in almost every fraud attempt
- Screen the five attempts small businesses see most: emails, texts, job offers, QR codes and invoices
- Verify any request through contact details you sourced yourself
- Report an attempt so it fails against your staff, your family and the next business
- Limit the damage if something gets through

## The lesson

### What the sender wants

Phishing is the trade name for a message that poses as someone you already deal with: your bank, a delivery company, a government office, a customer, or your own vendor. The sender wants one of three things: your money, your sign-in details, or access to your device. Attempts arrive by email, text, phone call, social media message and paper mail. The quality is high. Many have clean spelling and accurate logos, so a professional look proves nothing.

### The signals

The cover story changes. The signals stay the same.

- **Urgency or threats.** "Your account closes in 24 hours." "Final notice." Pressure is the main tool, because it pushes the check out of the process.
- **An unexpected request for money, codes, or sign-in details.** Especially payment by gift card, wire transfer, payment app, or cryptocurrency.
- **A link or attachment you were not expecting.**
- **A sender address that is slightly off.** The display name says your bank, but the actual address is a jumble or a misspelled version of the real one. Tap or click the sender's name to see the full address.
- **A change in payment details.** A different bank account or a different place to send money.
- **An offer that is too good.** A prize, a grant, or a refund you never applied for.
- **A request for secrecy.** "Do not tell anyone about this."

One signal alone is enough reason to verify before acting.

### Five attempts you will see

**Fake emails.** A message says there is a problem with your account and gives you a button to "verify" or "sign in." The button leads to a copy of the real sign-in page, and whatever is typed there goes to the sender. On a computer, rest your pointer on a link without clicking to see where it really goes.

**Fake texts.** A text says a package could not be delivered, a toll is unpaid, or your bank noticed a strange charge, and it includes a link. Real companies do send texts, which is why these work. Leave the link alone. Go to the company's app or website on your own.

**Fake job offers.** Someone offers work with high pay for little effort, often without a real interview. They may ask for payment up front for training or equipment, or they send a check, ask you to deposit it, and tell you to send part of it back. The check later bounces, and your bank takes the money back from you. Business owners see the same play from the other side: a "customer" overpays and asks for a refund of the difference.

**QR codes.** Fraudsters print their own codes and stick them over real ones on parking meters, flyers and menus, or send them in emails. When you scan a code, your phone shows a preview of the address. Read it before you tap. If you were not expecting the code, or the address looks wrong, do not open it.

**Fake invoices.** A bill arrives for something you never ordered, such as a listing in a directory, a website renewal, or office supplies. Or a message that appears to come from a real vendor asks you to pay a real bill to a different account. Match every invoice to something you ordered, and treat any change in payment details as a signal.

### The routine: stop, verify, report

**Stop.** Do not click, reply, call the number in the message, or pay. A real request will still be real in ten minutes.

**Verify.** Check the request through a contact you sourced yourself. Use the phone number on the back of your bank card, on a past statement, on your original contract, or on the official website you reach by typing the address or using your own bookmark. Never use the phone number, link, or reply button inside the suspicious message, because those lead back to the sender. Then ask directly: "Did you send this?"

**Report.** Mark the message as spam or phishing in your email or messaging app, and block the sender. Tell the real company it was impersonated. In the United States, you can report fraud to the Federal Trade Commission, and your state attorney general's office also takes reports. If you lost money or shared account details, contact your bank right away. Then brief your staff and family, because the same attempt is often run against the people around you.

### If something got through

These attempts are engineered to succeed against capable people, and some do. What counts is the next hour. Change that account's password, turn on two-step verification, and call your bank if money or card details were involved.

Set this as a standing rule for anyone who handles email or payments for you, and make clear that reporting an attempt is expected.

## Try it

All messages in this activity are simulated and use fictional names and businesses.

1. Open the sample messages in this lesson's downloads. There are five: an email, a text, a job offer, a QR code flyer, and an invoice.
2. For each one, circle or list every signal you find. Aim for at least two per message.
3. Decide for each message whether it is likely real, likely fraud, or needs checking.
4. Pick the fake vendor invoice. Write down where you would get the vendor's real phone number without using anything in the message.
5. Write the exact sentence you would say when you call to verify.
6. Write down who you would report it to. You now have five marked-up examples and a written stop, verify, report plan to keep by your desk.

## Where owners lose time or money

- **Relying on the display name.** Fix: open the full sender address and read it.
- **Calling the number in the message.** Fix: get the number from a source you already hold.
- **Treating polish as proof.** Fix: judge the request, not the look.
- **Moving at the speed the message demands.** Urgency is the signal. Fix: verify before anything is clicked or paid.
- **Keeping an attempt to yourself.** The same message goes to your staff next. Fix: report it and brief your team so the next attempt fails.

## Before you move on

- For your three largest vendors, do you hold a phone number on file that did not come from an email?
- Does everyone who opens email or pays bills for your business know the stop, verify, report routine?
- If a vendor emailed changed bank details today, what exactly would happen before a payment went out?

## What you walk away with

A working eye for the fraud attempts aimed at small businesses, and a routine to run every time before anyone in your business responds or clicks: stop, verify, report.
