# Recognizing Scams and Phishing

> Spot fake emails, texts, job offers, QR codes, and invoices, and use a simple routine: stop, verify, and report.

## Why this matters

Scammers go after small businesses on purpose. Your phone number and email are public, you are busy, and you handle money without a fraud department behind you. A cleaning company owner might get an email on a hectic Monday that looks like it came from her supply vendor: "Our bank details have changed. Please send this month's payment to the new account." The logo is right. The tone is right. If she pays it, the money is gone and the real vendor still needs to be paid. You do not need to be a technology expert to avoid this. You need to recognize the pattern and have a routine you follow every time.

## What you'll learn

- What phishing is and what scammers are really after
- The warning signs that show up in almost every scam
- What fake emails, texts, job offers, QR codes, and invoices look like
- How to verify a request using contact information you looked up yourself
- How and where to report a scam

## The lesson

### What phishing is

**Phishing** is a message that pretends to come from someone you trust in order to trick you into doing something. The sender may pose as your bank, a delivery company, a government office, a customer, or your own vendor. They want one of three things: your money, your sign-in details, or access to your device. Phishing arrives by email, text message, phone call, social media message, and even paper mail. Scam messages have also gotten better. Many now have clean spelling and convincing logos, so "it looks professional" is not proof of anything.

### The warning signs

Scams change their costumes, but the same signs keep showing up.

- **Urgency or threats.** "Your account closes in 24 hours." "Final notice." Pressure is the scammer's main tool, because rushed people skip checking.
- **An unexpected request for money, codes, or sign-in details.** Especially payment by gift card, wire transfer, payment app, or cryptocurrency.
- **A link or attachment you were not expecting.**
- **A sender address that is slightly off.** The display name says your bank, but the actual address is a jumble or a misspelled version of the real one. Tap or click the sender's name to see the full address.
- **A change in payment details.** A new bank account or a new place to send money.
- **An offer that is too good.** A prize, a grant, or a refund you never applied for.
- **A request for secrecy.** "Do not tell anyone about this."

One sign alone is enough reason to slow down.

### Five scams you will meet

**Fake emails.** A message says there is a problem with your account and gives you a button to "verify" or "sign in." The button leads to a copy of the real sign-in page, and whatever you type goes to the scammer. On a computer, you can rest your pointer on a link without clicking to see where it really goes.

**Fake texts.** A text says a package could not be delivered, a toll is unpaid, or your bank noticed a strange charge, and it includes a link. Real companies do send texts, which is what makes these work. Do not tap the link. Go to the company's app or website on your own.

**Fake job offers.** Someone offers work with high pay for little effort, often without a real interview. They may ask you to pay for training or equipment up front, or they send you a check, ask you to deposit it, and tell you to send part of it back. The check later bounces, and your bank takes the money back from you. Business owners see a version of this too: a "customer" overpays and asks for a refund of the difference.

**QR codes.** A **QR code** is a square pattern your phone camera reads to open a web page. Scammers print their own codes and stick them over real ones on parking meters, flyers, and menus, or send them in emails. When you scan a code, your phone shows a preview of the address. Read it before you tap. If you were not expecting the code, or the address looks wrong, do not open it.

**Fake invoices.** A bill arrives for something you never ordered, such as a listing in a directory, a website renewal, or office supplies. Or a message that appears to come from a real vendor asks you to pay a real bill to a new account. Match every invoice to something you actually ordered, and treat any change in payment details as a warning sign.

### The routine: stop, verify, report

**Stop.** Do not click, reply, call the number in the message, or pay. Take a breath. A real request will still be real in ten minutes.

**Verify.** Check the request through a contact you looked up yourself. Use the phone number on the back of your bank card, on a past statement, on your original contract, or on the official website you reach by typing the address or using your own bookmark. Never use the phone number, link, or reply button inside the suspicious message, because those lead back to the scammer. Then ask directly: "Did you send this?"

**Report.** Mark the message as spam or phishing in your email or messaging app, and block the sender. Tell the real company it was impersonated. In the United States, you can report fraud to the Federal Trade Commission, and your state attorney general's office also takes reports. If you lost money or shared account details, contact your bank right away. Finally, warn your staff and family, because scammers often try the same trick on the people around you.

If you already clicked or typed something in, act quickly: change that account's password, turn on two-step verification, and call your bank if money or card details were involved. Acting fast matters, and being fooled is nothing to be ashamed of.

## Try it

All messages in this activity are simulated and use fictional names and businesses.

1. Open the sample messages in this lesson's downloads. There are five: an email, a text, a job offer, a QR code flyer, and an invoice.
2. For each one, circle or list every warning sign you find. Aim for at least two per message.
3. Decide for each message whether it is likely real, likely a scam, or needs checking.
4. Pick the fake vendor invoice. Write down where you would find the vendor's real phone number without using anything in the message.
5. Write the exact sentence you would say when you call to verify.
6. Write down who you would report it to. You now have five marked-up examples and a written stop, verify, report plan you can keep by your desk.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting the display name.** Open the full sender address and read it.
- **Calling the number in the message.** Look up the number yourself from a source you already trust.
- **Thinking good spelling means it is real.** Judge the request, not the polish.
- **Acting fast because the message says to.** Urgency is the warning sign. Slow down.
- **Staying quiet out of embarrassment.** Report it and tell your team so the next attempt fails.

## What you walk away with

You can recognize the common scams aimed at small businesses, and you have a routine to follow every time: stop, verify, and report before you respond or click.
