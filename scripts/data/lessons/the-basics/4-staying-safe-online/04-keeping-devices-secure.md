# Keeping Devices Secure

> Use updates, screen locks, trusted apps, backups, and careful habits on shared devices and public Wi-Fi to protect your business.

## Why this matters

Your phone and laptop hold your customer list, your messages, your photos, your banking app, and the email account that unlocks everything else. If a device is lost, stolen, or infected, your business can stop cold. Think of a mobile notary who leaves her phone in a ride share. With a screen lock and a backup, it is an annoying afternoon: she locks the phone from another device, buys a replacement, and restores her contacts and appointments. Without them, a stranger can open her email and payment app, and three years of client contacts are simply gone. Device security is a few settings and habits you set up once and then keep.

## What you'll learn

- Why software updates matter and how to turn on automatic updates
- How to set a screen lock and why it is your first line of defense
- How to install apps from trusted sources and review their permissions
- How to back up your files so a lost device is not a lost business
- How to stay safe on shared devices and public Wi-Fi

## The lesson

### Software updates

Software always has flaws, and criminals look for them. When a company finds a flaw, it sends out a fix in an **update**. A device that has not been updated still has the flaw, and attackers know exactly where it is. That is why "Remind me later" for months at a time is risky. Updates apply to the operating system, your apps, and your browser.

The easy answer is to turn on **automatic updates**. Open **Settings** and look for **Software Update**, **System Update**, or **Windows Update**, and switch on the automatic option. Do the same in your app store's settings. Then restart your device when it asks, because many updates do not finish until you do. One caution: real updates come from your device's settings or its app store. A pop-up on a website saying "Your device is infected, click here to update" is a scam. Close the browser tab.

### Screen locks

A **screen lock** requires a PIN, password, fingerprint, or face scan before the device opens. It is what stands between a thief and everything on the device. Set one on every phone, tablet, and computer you use for business. In Settings, look under **Security**, **Lock screen**, **Face ID and Passcode**, or **Sign-in options**. Use a PIN of at least six digits that is not a birthday or a simple pattern such as 123456. Then set the screen to lock by itself after a short time, a minute or two for a phone and a few minutes for a computer.

Two related settings are worth turning on. The first is the built-in **find my device** feature, which lets you locate, lock, or erase a lost device from another device. The second is limiting what shows on the lock screen, so message previews and verification codes are not readable by anyone who picks up your phone.

### Trusted app sources and permissions

Install apps only from the official store for your device, such as the App Store on an iPhone, Google Play on Android, or the Microsoft Store or the software maker's own website on a computer. Apps offered through links in texts, emails, or pop-ups may carry **malware**, which is software built to steal information or damage a device. Even in an official store, check before you install: read the developer's name, look at recent reviews, and be suspicious of copycats with names close to a well-known app.

**Permissions** are what an app is allowed to reach on your device, such as your camera, microphone, location, contacts, and photos. A permission should make sense for what the app does. A map app needs your location. A flashlight app does not need your contacts. To review them, open **Settings** and look for **Privacy**, **Apps**, or **Permissions**. Turn off anything that does not fit, and delete apps you no longer use.

### Backups

A **backup** is a second copy of your files kept somewhere other than the device. Devices get dropped, stolen, soaked, and worn out. A backup turns a disaster into an inconvenience. You have two main choices, and using both is best. A **cloud backup** copies your files to your online storage account automatically. An **external drive** is a small storage device you plug into a computer to copy files onto, then store somewhere safe.

Phones can back up automatically. Look in Settings under your account name, or under **Backup**, and switch it on. On a computer, the built-in backup tools are in Settings as well. Whatever method you use, test it once by opening a backed-up file from a different device. A backup you have never tested is only a hope.

### Shared devices and public Wi-Fi

A **shared device** is any computer other people also use, such as one at a library, a hotel, or a family computer at home. On a shared device, use a private or incognito browser window, never let the browser save your password, and sign out of every account before you close the window. Avoid banking on a computer you do not control. If staff share a business computer, give each person a separate user account where you can.

**Public Wi-Fi** in coffee shops, airports, and hotels is open to anyone, and you cannot be sure who runs it or who else is on it. Confirm the exact network name with an employee, because scammers set up look-alike networks. Turn off the setting that joins open networks automatically. Save banking and other sensitive tasks for a network you trust. When you need a safer connection on the go, your phone's cellular data or its **hotspot** feature, which shares your phone's connection with your laptop, is the better choice. Lock your screen whenever you step away, even for a moment.

## Try it

1. Open **Settings** on your own device and find the software update screen. Note whether automatic updates are on, and turn them on if they are not.
2. Find the screen lock settings. Confirm a lock is set and note how long before the screen locks by itself.
3. Find where the find my device feature is and check whether it is on.
4. Open the app permissions list. Pick three apps and look at what each can reach. Turn off one permission that does not make sense.
5. Create a short practice file with nothing sensitive in it, such as a note that says "Backup test." Save a copy to your cloud storage or an external drive.
6. Open that copy from the backup location to prove it is there.
7. Mark each item on the device security checklist in this lesson's downloads. You now have a completed checklist showing what is protected and what you still need to set up.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting off updates for weeks.** Turn on automatic updates and restart when asked.
- **Using no screen lock, or a PIN such as 1234.** Set a six-digit PIN or longer that is not a date or pattern.
- **Clicking a pop-up that says your device is infected.** Close the tab. Update only through Settings or the official store.
- **Assuming files are backed up.** Check the backup setting and open one backed-up file to confirm.
- **Staying signed in on a shared computer.** Sign out of every account and close the browser before you leave.
