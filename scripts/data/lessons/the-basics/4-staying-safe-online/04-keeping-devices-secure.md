# Make a Lost or Hacked Device a Minor Problem

> Set up automatic updates, screen locks, trusted apps and tested backups once, and handle shared devices and public Wi-Fi without exposing the business.

## Why this matters

Your phone and laptop hold your customer list, your messages, your photos, your banking app, and the email account that unlocks everything else. If a device is lost, stolen, or infected, the business can stop cold. A mobile notary leaves her phone in a ride share after a long day of appointments. With a screen lock and a backup, it costs her an afternoon: she locks the phone from another device, buys a replacement, and restores her contacts and appointments. Without them, a stranger can open her email and payment app, and three years of client contacts are gone. Device security is a short list of settings you configure once and a few habits you keep.

## What you'll be able to do

- Turn on automatic updates so known flaws get closed without your attention
- Set a screen lock, remote locate and erase, and private lock-screen notifications
- Install apps from trusted sources and trim their permissions
- Back up your files and confirm the backup restores
- Work on shared devices and public Wi-Fi without leaving accounts exposed

## The lesson

### Updates

Software always has flaws, and criminals look for them. When a company finds one, it sends out a fix in an update. A device that has not been updated still has the flaw, and attackers know exactly where it is. That is why tapping "Remind me later" for months is a real exposure. Updates apply to the operating system, your apps and your browser.

Set this up once and it runs itself. Open **Settings**, go to **Software Update**, **System Update**, or **Windows Update**, and switch on the automatic option. Do the same in your app store's settings. Restart when the device asks, because many updates do not finish until you do.

Real updates come from your device's settings or its app store. A pop-up on a website saying "Your device is infected, click here to update" is fraud. Close the browser tab.

### Screen locks

The screen lock is what stands between a thief and everything on the device. Set one on every phone, tablet and computer you use for business. In Settings, look under **Security**, **Lock screen**, **Face ID and Passcode**, or **Sign-in options**. Use a PIN of at least six digits that is not a birthday or a pattern such as 123456. Then set the screen to lock by itself after a short time: a minute or two for a phone, a few minutes for a computer.

Two related settings are worth turning on.

- **Find my device.** The built-in feature that lets you locate, lock, or erase a lost device from another device.
- **Lock-screen previews.** Limit what shows on the lock screen, so message previews and verification codes are not readable by whoever picks up your phone.

### Trusted app sources and permissions

Install apps only from the official store for your device: the App Store on an iPhone, Google Play on Android, or the Microsoft Store or the software maker's own website on a computer. Apps offered through links in texts, emails, or pop-ups may carry malware, software built to steal information or damage a device. In an official store, still check before you install: read the developer's name, look at recent reviews, and watch for copycats with names close to a well-known app.

Permissions are what an app is allowed to reach on your device: camera, microphone, location, contacts and photos. A permission should fit what the app does. A map app needs your location. A flashlight app does not need your contacts. To review them, open **Settings** and go to **Privacy**, **Apps**, or **Permissions**. Turn off anything that does not fit, and delete apps you no longer use.

### Backups

Devices get dropped, stolen, soaked and worn out. A second copy of your files, kept somewhere other than the device, turns a disaster into an inconvenience. You have two main choices, and using both is best.

- **Cloud backup.** Copies your files to your online storage account automatically.
- **External drive.** A storage device you plug into a computer to copy files onto, then store somewhere safe.

Phones can back up automatically. Look in Settings under your account name, or under **Backup**, and switch it on. On a computer, the built-in backup tools are in Settings as well. Whatever method you use, test it once by opening a backed-up file from a different device. A backup you have never tested is only a hope.

### Shared devices

On any computer other people also use, such as one at a library, a hotel, or a family computer at home:

- Use a private or incognito browser window.
- Never let the browser save your password.
- Sign out of every account before you close the window.
- Avoid banking on a computer you do not control.

If staff share a business computer, give each person a separate user account where you can.

### Public Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi in coffee shops, airports and hotels is open to the public, and you cannot be sure who runs it or who else is on it.

- Confirm the exact network name with an employee, because fraudsters set up look-alike networks.
- Turn off the setting that joins open networks automatically.
- Save banking and other sensitive tasks for a network you trust.
- When you need a safer connection on the go, use your phone's cellular data or its hotspot, which shares your phone's connection with your laptop.
- Lock your screen whenever you step away, even for a moment.

## Try it

1. Open **Settings** on your own device and go to the software update screen. Note whether automatic updates are on, and turn them on if they are not.
2. Open the screen lock settings. Confirm a lock is set and note how long before the screen locks by itself.
3. Locate the find my device feature and check whether it is on.
4. Open the app permissions list. Pick three apps and look at what each can reach. Turn off one permission that does not fit.
5. Create a short practice file with nothing sensitive in it, such as a note that says "Backup test." Save a copy to your cloud storage or an external drive.
6. Open that copy from the backup location to prove it is there.
7. Mark each item on the device security checklist in this lesson's downloads. You now have a completed checklist showing what is protected and what you still need to set up.

## Where owners lose time or money

- **Putting off updates for weeks.** The flaw stays open the whole time. Fix: turn on automatic updates and restart when asked.
- **No screen lock, or a PIN such as 1234.** A lost phone then hands over email, banking and client contacts. Fix: set a six-digit PIN or longer that is not a date or pattern.
- **Clicking a pop-up that says your device is infected.** That click is the infection. Fix: close the tab. Update only through Settings or the official store.
- **Assuming files are backed up.** Many owners find out the setting was off on the day the device is gone. Fix: check the backup setting and open one backed-up file to confirm.
- **Staying signed in on a shared computer.** Fix: sign out of every account and close the browser before you leave.

## Before you move on

- If your phone disappeared today, could you lock or erase it from another device within minutes?
- When did you last open a backed-up file from a different device, and are your client contacts in that backup?
- Are automatic updates on for every phone, tablet and computer the business uses?
- Can someone holding your locked phone read your verification codes on the lock screen?
- Does each person who uses a shared business computer have a separate user account?

## What you walk away with

A device security checklist filled in for your own device, covering updates, screen lock, apps, backups, and working habits for shared devices and public networks.
