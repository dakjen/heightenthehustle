# Passwords and Two-Step Verification

> Build long, unique passwords, let a password manager remember them, and add a second lock to every important account.

## Why this matters

Your business lives inside a handful of accounts: your email, your bank, your payment app, your social media pages, your booking system. Whoever gets into your email can reset the passwords to almost everything else. Picture a barbershop owner who uses one short password for everything. A website he signed up for years ago gets broken into, and that password leaks. A stranger tries it on his email, and it works. Within an hour the stranger controls his booking page and his business social media account, and is messaging his customers asking for money. Two habits would have stopped it: a different password for each account, and a second step at sign-in.

## What you'll learn

- Why length and uniqueness matter more than fancy symbols
- How to build a passphrase you can actually remember
- What a password manager does and how it helps
- What two-step verification is and how to turn it on
- How to store recovery codes safely

## The lesson

### Long and unique

Two things make a password strong. The first is **length**. Attackers use software that guesses passwords at very high speed, and every extra character makes the guessing far harder. A short password with a symbol swapped in, such as an "a" changed to "@", is still short, and guessing software knows those tricks. The second is **uniqueness**, meaning you use each password for one account only. Companies get broken into, and lists of stolen passwords get passed around. If you reuse a password, one break-in anywhere opens every account that shares it.

### Passphrases

A **passphrase** is a password made of several unrelated words, such as four or five random words strung together. It is long, which makes it strong, and it is made of real words, which makes it easier to remember and type. Choose words that have nothing to do with each other or with you. Avoid anything a person could learn about you: names of family or pets, birthdays, your street, your business name, your team. Avoid famous quotes and song lines too, because guessing software includes those. If a site requires a number or symbol, add one between words.

An example of the pattern, for illustration only: **copper-lantern-seventeen-walrus-picnic**. Never use an example you saw in a lesson as a real password.

### Password managers

Nobody can remember a different long password for thirty accounts. A **password manager** is an app that stores all your passwords in a locked vault. You remember one strong passphrase, called the main or master password, and the manager remembers everything else. It can also create long random passwords for you and fill them in when you sign in.

Phones, computers, and browsers come with a password manager built in, and there are separate apps as well. Either kind is far safer than reusing passwords or keeping them in a notes app or an unlocked spreadsheet. Protect the manager itself with your best passphrase and with two-step verification. If you prefer paper, a notebook kept locked up at home is a reasonable choice. A sticky note on the register is not.

### Two-step verification

**Two-step verification** adds a second lock. After your password, the account asks for a second proof that it is really you. It may also be called two-factor authentication, 2FA, or multi-factor authentication. The idea is that a thief who steals your password still cannot get in without the second step.

Common second steps include a code sent by text message, a code from an **authenticator app** on your phone, a prompt on your phone that you tap to approve, or a small physical security key. A text code is much better than nothing. An authenticator app or a prompt is stronger, because text messages can sometimes be redirected by criminals. One rule covers all of them: **a real company will never call, text, or email you to ask for your code.** If anyone asks you to read a code to them, it is a scam. And if you get a code or an approval prompt you did not ask for, do not approve it. Someone may have your password, so change it.

Turning it on follows the same pattern on most accounts.

1. Sign in and open **Settings**.
2. Look for a section called **Security**, **Sign-in**, or **Password and security**.
3. Choose **Two-step verification** or **Two-factor authentication** and turn it on.
4. Pick your second step and follow the prompts.
5. Save the recovery codes it offers you.

Start with your email, then your bank and payment apps, then social media.

### Recovery codes

When you turn on two-step verification, most accounts give you a set of **recovery codes**, also called backup codes. Each one works once and gets you in if your phone is lost or broken. Treat them like spare keys. Print them or write them down and keep them somewhere locked, or store them in your password manager. Do not keep them only on the phone they are meant to replace, and do not email them to yourself. Also check that the recovery phone number and recovery email on each account are current.

## Try it

All practice uses the demonstration account for this course and made-up information. Do not type, say, or show a real password at any point.

1. On paper, write three sample passphrases using the pattern of four or five unrelated words. These are practice only. Do not use them on real accounts.
2. Cross out any that contain something about you, and replace them.
3. Sign in to the demonstration account with the practice details provided in class.
4. Open its settings, find the security section, and turn on two-step verification.
5. View the demonstration recovery codes and decide where you would store real ones: printed and locked up, or in a password manager.
6. Fill in the account security checklist in this lesson's downloads, listing your important accounts by name only and marking which need a new unique password and which need two-step verification. You now have a personal checklist to work through privately at home.

## Common mistakes

- **Using one password for many accounts.** Give every account its own, and let a password manager remember them.
- **Making a short password "complex."** Go longer instead. A passphrase beats a short password with symbols.
- **Sharing a verification code with a caller.** Never share a code with anyone who contacts you. Hang up.
- **Skipping recovery codes.** Save them the same day you turn on two-step verification.
- **Sharing one login with staff.** Give each person their own account where the service allows it, and change passwords when someone leaves.
