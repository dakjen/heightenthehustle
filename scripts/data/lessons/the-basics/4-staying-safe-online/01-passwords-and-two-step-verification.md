# Lock Down the Accounts Your Business Runs On

> Give every account its own long passphrase, let a password manager hold them, and put a second lock on email, banking and payments.

## Why this matters

Your business lives inside a handful of accounts: your email, your bank, your payment app, your social media pages, your booking system. Whoever gets into your email can reset the passwords to almost everything else. Account takeover rarely starts with anything the owner did that day. A barbershop owner signed up years ago for a website that later gets broken into, and the password he used there leaks. A stranger tries the same password on his email, and it works. Within an hour the stranger controls his booking page and his business social media account, and is messaging his customers asking for money. Two controls stop that chain: a different password for each account, and a second step at sign-in.

## What you'll be able to do

- Build passphrases that hold up against guessing software
- Run every business login through a password manager so nothing is reused
- Turn on two-step verification, starting with the accounts that hold the most
- Store recovery codes so a lost phone does not lock you out of your own business
- Give staff access without sharing a login

## The lesson

### Long and unique

Two things make a password strong. The first is **length**. Attackers use software that guesses passwords at very high speed, and every extra character makes the guessing far harder. A short password with a symbol swapped in, such as an "a" changed to "@", is still short, and guessing software knows those tricks. The second is **uniqueness**: each password is used for one account only. Companies get broken into, and lists of stolen passwords get passed around. A reused password means one break-in anywhere opens every account that shares it.

### Build a passphrase

A passphrase is several unrelated words strung together, four or five of them. It is long, which makes it strong, and it is made of real words, which makes it quicker to remember and type. Choose words that have nothing to do with each other or with you. Leave out anything a person could find out about you: names of family or pets, birthdays, your street, your business name, your team. Leave out famous quotes and song lines too, because guessing software includes those. If a site requires a number or symbol, add one between words.

An example of the pattern, for illustration only: **copper-lantern-seventeen-walrus-picnic**. Never use an example you saw in a lesson as a real password.

### Let a password manager carry the load

Nobody can remember a different long password for thirty accounts. A password manager stores all of them in a locked vault. You remember one strong passphrase, called the main or master password, and the manager remembers everything else. It also creates long random passwords for you and fills them in when you sign in.

Phones, computers and browsers come with a password manager built in, and there are separate apps as well. Either kind is far safer than reusing passwords or keeping them in a notes app or an unlocked spreadsheet. Protect the manager itself with your best passphrase and with two-step verification. If you prefer paper, a notebook kept locked up at home is a reasonable choice. A sticky note on the register is not.

### Add a second lock

Two-step verification asks for a second proof after your password, so a thief who has the password still cannot get in. Settings menus also call it two-factor authentication, 2FA, or multi-factor authentication.

Common second steps:

- A code sent by text message
- A code from an authenticator app on your phone
- A prompt on your phone that you tap to approve
- A small physical security key

A text code is much better than nothing. An authenticator app or a prompt is stronger, because text messages can sometimes be redirected by criminals.

One rule covers all of them: **a real company will never call, text, or email you to ask for your code.** Anyone who asks you to read a code to them is running a scam. And if a code or an approval prompt arrives that you did not ask for, do not approve it. Someone may have your password, so change it.

Turning it on follows the same pattern on most accounts.

1. Sign in and open **Settings**.
2. Go to the section called **Security**, **Sign-in**, or **Password and security**.
3. Choose **Two-step verification** or **Two-factor authentication** and turn it on.
4. Pick your second step and follow the prompts.
5. Save the recovery codes it offers you.

Work in order of what a thief could do with each account: email, then your bank and payment apps, then social media.

### Recovery codes

When you turn on two-step verification, most accounts give you a set of recovery codes, also called backup codes. Each one works once and gets you in if your phone is lost or broken. Treat them like spare keys.

- Print them or write them down and keep them somewhere locked, or store them in your password manager.
- Do not keep them only on the phone they are meant to replace.
- Do not email them to yourself.
- Confirm that the recovery phone number and recovery email on each account are current.

### Staff access

A shared login cannot be traced to a person and cannot be taken back from one person without changing it for everyone. Give each person their own account where the service allows it, with only the access their job needs. When someone leaves, remove their account the same day and change any password they knew.

## Try it

All practice uses the demonstration account for this course and made-up information. Do not type, say, or show a real password at any point.

1. On paper, write three sample passphrases using the pattern of four or five unrelated words. These are practice only. Do not use them on real accounts.
2. Cross out any that contain something about you, and replace them.
3. Sign in to the demonstration account with the practice details provided in class.
4. Open its settings, go to the security section, and turn on two-step verification.
5. View the demonstration recovery codes and decide where you would store real ones: printed and locked up, or in a password manager.
6. Fill in the account security checklist in this lesson's downloads, listing your important accounts by name only and marking which need a unique password and which need two-step verification. You now have a personal checklist to work through privately at home.

## Where owners lose time or money

- **One password across many accounts.** A leak at any one company opens all of them. Fix: give every account its own, and let a password manager remember them.
- **A short password made "complex."** Fix: go longer. A passphrase beats a short password with symbols.
- **Reading a verification code to a caller.** That code is the last lock on the account. Fix: never share a code with anyone who contacts you. Hang up.
- **Skipping recovery codes.** A broken phone then means days locked out of email or banking. Fix: save them the same day you turn on two-step verification.
- **One login shared with staff.** You cannot tell who did what, and a departure leaves the door open. Fix: separate accounts where the service allows it, and change passwords when someone leaves.

## Before you move on

- Does your business email have its own passphrase, used nowhere else, and two-step verification turned on?
- If your phone broke today, could you still get into your email and bank with recovery codes you can reach?
- Are your logins in a password manager or a locked notebook, with none in a notes app, spreadsheet or on a sticky note?
- Does anyone who no longer works with you still know a password that works?

## What you walk away with

A method for building strong passphrases, a clear order for adding a second lock to each account, and a checklist for securing your own accounts, completed without sharing a password in class.
