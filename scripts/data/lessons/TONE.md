# Tone rules for HTH lessons

The reader is an adult who already runs a business (catering, cleaning, barbering, childcare, trades, retail). They use a smartphone all day, they are sharp, and they are short on time. A reviewer said earlier drafts were patronizing: language like "what is a computer" "makes a person sound stupid" and "might turn somebody off". Write so nobody could feel talked down to.

## Do

- Write peer to peer, the way one experienced business owner briefs another. Direct, specific, respectful of their time.
- Start every section from a business task or result (send the quote, get paid, find the file in ten seconds), not from a definition.
- Assume everyday knowledge. They know what a screen, keyboard, app, website, email, file, password and Wi-Fi are. Do not explain those.
- When a genuinely technical or trade term is needed (reconcile, owner's draw, two-step verification, cell reference), use it and gloss it in a few words in passing, inside a sentence about doing something. Never give it its own "What X is" section.
- Replace space freed by cutting basics with material that is worth their time: faster ways of working, what professionals do, how to avoid losing money or a customer, what to set up once so it runs itself.
- Present skills as how to get more out of a tool they already have, never as learning it for the first time.

## Do not

- No headings, titles, learning goals or quiz questions of the form "What is a ...", "What X is", "Understanding ...", "Your first ...", "Getting started with ...", "the basics of ...".
- No words that label the reader: beginner, new to, first time, never used, basic(s), simple/simply, easy/easily, just (as a minimiser), "anyone can".
- No reassurance or emotion coaching: "don't worry", "it's okay", "nothing to be embarrassed about", "you stop feeling stuck", "this can feel overwhelming", "you can do this", praise such as "great job".
- No childish memory tricks or analogies ("if you can drop it on your foot it is hardware").
- No instructions to locate obvious things ("find the power button on your device").
- No school-quiz tone. The "Check yourself" section becomes "Before you move on": 3 to 5 practical checks about their own business ("Can you pull last month's total in under a minute?"), not definition questions.
- "Common mistakes" becomes "Where owners lose time or money": state the costly habit and the fix, without implying the reader is careless.
- Do not make the fictional example owners look helpless or foolish. They are competent people solving real problems.

## Keep

- File format exactly: line 1 `# Title`, blank line, line 3 `> one-line summary`, blank line, then the lesson in Markdown with `##` sections.
- Sections, in order: `## Why this matters`, `## What you'll be able to do`, `## The lesson` (with `###` subsections), `## Try it` (or `## Do this now`), `## Where owners lose time or money`, `## Before you move on`, `## What you walk away with`.
- Every teaching point, step, figure and example business already in the lesson, unless it is one of the obvious-knowledge explanations being cut. Do not invent new statistics, prices of products, laws or URLs. No links to outside websites. No names of real people.
- Accurate click-by-click steps for the tool. Steps are fine and are not patronizing. Explaining what a mouse is, is.
- Length: 1,000 to 1,400 words per lesson. Never shorter than 950.
- Plain language, short sentences, no em dashes, no semicolons at the end of a line, never the text `$md$`.
- Titles: retitle any that sound remedial so they name the result (for example "Understanding Your Computer and Phone" could become "Fix Device Problems Fast and Get the Right Help"). Titles must stay unique within the course. Update the summary line to match. Do not rename files or folders.
